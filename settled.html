Layer 1 and 2 · the part nobody argues about

The settled floor

Most arguments about the micronova never reach the ground floor. The dismissal arrives so early that nobody surveys what has already been conceded, and the conversation carries on as though the frontier were claiming things science denies. On these five, science does not deny them. It publishes them.

One

The Sun does things we have never measured.

In 2012 a researcher named Fusa Miyake found something odd in the rings of Japanese cedar: a sharp jump in radioactive carbon across a single year, 774 into 775. That jump has since turned up in tree rings, coral and ice from around the world. The cause is a burst of particles from the Sun roughly ten times past the 1859 Carrington storm, which is the biggest event our instruments have ever seen.

About ten of these have been found across the last 15,000 years. The reason it matters: 1859 is the usual benchmark for "worst case", and it is not the worst case. It is the worst case of a very short sample.

Mind's eye

If the 1859 storm was the king tide that put water over the road, these are the ones that put it through the house. And we have only been keeping the tide book for 170 years.

Two

And the sky keeps a longer record than the instruments do.

There is a second way to count big storms, and it reaches back centuries further than any machine. When a storm is severe enough, the aurora stops being a polar thing and comes down over the tropics, and people write that down. Chronicles, diaries, ships' logs, newspapers. Read enough of them and you can measure storms that happened long before anyone had an instrument to measure them with.

The work is painstaking and it has been done. More than seven hundred separate auroral records were tracked down for one February 1872 storm alone, from libraries and archives and observatories around the world. For a September 1770 storm, a hundred and eleven documents across East Asia describe low-latitude displays running for nine nights straight.

The last two rows, together

May 2024 was the storm that moved half the satellites in low orbit, emptied the ionosphere to a record degree and had grid operators watching voltage all weekend. It measured about -412. Three of the storms above it on that list are somewhere between twice and four times its size, and all three happened after 1770. The event that stressed the modern world in 2024 was not a big one. It was a moderate one that arrived in a world with a great deal more plugged in.

And two words that must not be swapped

A storm the size of Carrington and an aurora reaching as far as Carrington's are not the same thing, and treating them as one is the most common error in this whole area. May 2024 produced an auroral reach in the same neighbourhood as the great historical storms while measuring roughly a third to a half of their strength. That gap is the interesting part, and it wants an explanation rather than a shrug.

The leading explanation is not exotic and it is published: the aurora comes further down when the field is weaker. The ring of light sits where the field lines open out, and as the dipole loses strength that ring widens and slides toward the equator. Put the other way round, and this is how the literature phrases it: a stronger field would have needed a stronger storm to push the aurora to the same place on the map.

Which turns the aurora into an instrument

If a given storm now paints further south than the same storm would have in 1859, then how far down the light comes is partly a reading of the shield rather than only a reading of the Sun. And there is a corollary that runs the other way and matters: the storms that reached twenty degrees in 1770 and 1872 did it against a stronger field than today's. To get that far down through a thicker shield, they had to be more extreme than the raw comparison with 2024 already suggests.

Held honestly, because it is not a clean global relation. The field is not weakening evenly, so the northern and southern rings are not moving in step, and at least one study finds the northern one has been shrinking rather than spreading. Any use of auroral reach as a measure of the shield has to be regional, not global.

The confound that cuts the other way, and it is a real one

May 2024 was the first solar maximum with a phone in every pocket. Phone sensors pick up colour the eye simply cannot, and a great many low-latitude reports from that week were pale smudges that only became green or red on a screen. Every historical entry in the table above is naked-eye. So the modern rows are not measured the same way as the old ones, and any comparison that ignores that is inflating the present. The fix is to compare naked-eye reports with naked-eye reports and say which is which.

On the third possibility, that we are changing the upper atmosphere ourselves: something in that class is documented and it is not the facility usually named, but it concerns where the radiation belts begin rather than how far south the lights come. It is set out here.

Count with instruments and you get one Carrington. Count with the sky and you get at least four, and one of them may have been bigger.

And that table goes stale fast, which is its own lesson

Since May 2024 there have been at least five more storms throwing the aurora well outside its usual country. A severe one in October 2024, on the back of the largest flare of that cycle. A severe one in November 2025, off an X5 flare, with displays over Arizona, Texas, Florida and into Europe. A severe one on 19 January 2026, forecast at the time as possibly reaching the top category. A strong one in early July 2026, with magenta light over northern California and the aurora reaching New Mexico. And another severe one in late July 2026, with more waves behind it.

Five events in a little over two years, all of them putting the aurora over places that do not normally see it. Not one of them was anywhere near the size of the historical entries above. That is what an active Sun looks like from underneath, and it is worth knowing before deciding the quiet is normal.

There is a further thread worth pulling. Auroral records from the 990s have been read as evidence of great magnetic storms in that decade, and 993 is one of the confirmed tree-ring spikes. Two completely independent archives, written and physical, pointing at the same years.

Known

Three

Stars like ours flare, about once a century.

In December 2024 a team led by Valeriy Vasilyev published in Science. They went through Kepler telescope data on 56,450 stars with the Sun's temperature and behaviour, and found 2,889 superflares on 2,527 of them. The rate works out at roughly one per star per century, which is ten to a hundred times more often than astronomy had estimated.

The paper's own words: if the Sun behaves like the stars in the sample, it could produce superflares at a similar rate.

This is the single most useful fact in the whole argument, and it did not come from the frontier. It came from the mainstream correcting itself, in the most conventional venue available, and it removes "the Sun does not do that" from the table. It does not establish a micronova. It raises the ceiling on what the Sun is known to do, and moves it closer.

Four

The shield fails, and comes back.

A geomagnetic excursion is not a flip. The field weakens sharply, the poles wander a long way from where they belong, and then it recovers to the same polarity it had before. Confusing an excursion with a full reversal is the most common mistake in this argument, and both sides make it.

About thirteen of these are well documented across the last 780,000 years, and the five most studied are Mono Lake, Laschamp, Blake, Iceland Basin and Pringle Falls. The planet's magnetic shield is not a constant. It has failed, more than once, while people were alive to see it.

One distinction carries a lot of weight further on. An excursion being proposed is not the same as it being accepted. A 2020 review lists seven more put forward for the last 30,000 years alone that have never made it into the inventory, and three of those are rungs on the cadence ladder.

Five

It is weakening now, and it broke a map.

Every phone, ship and aircraft that finds north uses a model of the Earth's magnetic field, updated on a five-year cycle. In 2019 that model had to be updated early, for the first time ever, because the north magnetic pole had gone somewhere the model did not expect. That is not a small administrative event. It is the navigation system of the world admitting it had been outrun.

Two things that happened after most of this argument was written down

In May 2024 the strongest geomagnetic storm in over twenty years arrived, the first in the severe category since 2003. Roughly half of the ten thousand satellites in low orbit had to manoeuvre, the largest such movement on record, and one was dragged down eleven days early. Grid operators spent the weekend managing voltage. Nothing failed, and it was nowhere near the top of what the Sun can do.

And the forecast for the current solar cycle was wrong in a specific direction. The official panel called for a weak cycle peaking around a sunspot number of 115 in mid-2025. The peak actually came in October 2024 at about 161, with counts at a twenty-three year high and an X9 flare that same month. The estimate had to be revised upward twice.

Both halves of that table belong in an honest answer. The changes are real and were strange enough to break a working model. The numbers are also smaller than the frontier states, and the pole is slowing rather than speeding up. Anyone arguing this without carrying the second half will get corrected in public by someone with a search engine.

Six

Micronova is a real word.

In April 2022 Nature published Scaringi and colleagues on hours-long thermonuclear bursts confined to the magnetic poles of collapsed stars. The European Southern Observatory announced it as a new kind of stellar explosion, and named it the micronova. The frontier had been using the word for years before that.

So the chronology is real, and it is regularly denied. It is also regularly over-claimed. The 2022 object is a dead star's surface burning fuel stolen from a companion, and none of that physics carries across to a living star like ours. Sharing a name is not sharing a mechanism.

What the floor buys

And what it does not.

It buys

That the Sun has a much bigger tail than our instruments have seen. That the magnetic shield fails periodically. That it is weakening now. And that the argument about the word is settled in the frontier's favour, as a matter of who said it first.

It does not buy

A 12,000-year clock. A trigger from outside the Solar System. The crust coming loose. A shell thrown off the Sun. Those sit on the upper layers and have to be argued there, on their own evidence, which is thinner.

Anyone who says the floor is empty has not looked. Anyone who treats the floor as the whole building is not being careful either.

The strongest argument here, and the least contested

We have not been safe. We have been lucky.

Nothing in this section needs a micronova, an excursion or a cycle. It is ordinary space weather statistics, and it is the part that should change what anyone does on Monday.

Storms of the kind that would take a modern grid apart are not rare in the Sun's terms. They turn up roughly every solar maximum. What has saved us so far is not scarcity. It is aim.

And the aim is worse than most people assume

A big eruption leaves from wherever the active region happens to be sitting. The published arrival work finds that the ones which reach Earth come from a band of about eighty degrees of solar longitude, and that roughly three in five launched from inside that band actually arrive.

Eighty degrees out of three hundred and sixty is about a fifth of the Sun. Multiply the two and a large eruption has something like a one in eight chance of being pointed at us. Not one in a thousand. One in eight. And the Sun turns once a month, so a long-lived active region sweeps that band again, and again, for as long as it lasts.

Held honestly, because the numbers have argument in them

The eighty-degree window is longitude only. Latitude matters as well, since Earth sits within about seven degrees of the solar equator and active regions drift equatorward through a cycle, so the real figure moves with cycle phase. Treat one in eight as an order of magnitude, not a precision number.

The per-decade rate has been argued over and revised, with published estimates running from well under one per cent up to twelve depending on method. The table takes the high end. At the low end a century still comes out around one in twenty, which is not a comfortable number either.

And one assumption underneath all of it, which is not settled

The arithmetic above assumes that where an eruption happens on the Sun has nothing to do with where Earth is. That is an assumption rather than a finding, and it is worth naming because it is contested.

Whether the planets influence solar activity is a live published question. The Venus, Earth and Jupiter alignment period of 11.07 years tracks the eleven-year sunspot cycle closely, tidally synchronised dynamo models have appeared in mainstream solar physics journals, and there was a full review of the planetary theory in 2022 with work continuing since, including a paper on tidal triggers that discusses the Carrington event directly. What nobody has settled is the mechanism, because the tidal forces involved are extremely weak and how they could pace a dynamo is exactly the open part.

One distinction matters here. Those models concern when the Sun is active, not which face erupts, and cycle timing would not by itself tilt the longitude. But Earth is one of the three bodies named, so "the Sun does not know where Earth is" claims more than anyone has shown. It is also checkable on public data, and that check is on the list.

What holds regardless: nobody has produced a mechanism that aims these away from us, and neither the geometry nor the rotation is in dispute. Neither of those runs out of patience.
