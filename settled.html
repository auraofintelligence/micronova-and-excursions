Layer 1 and 2 · the part nobody argues about

The settled floor

Most arguments about the micronova never reach the ground floor. The dismissal arrives so early that nobody surveys what has already been conceded, and the conversation carries on as though the frontier were claiming things science denies. On these five, science does not deny them. It publishes them.

One

The Sun does things we have never measured.

In 2012 a researcher named Fusa Miyake found something odd in the rings of Japanese cedar: a sharp jump in radioactive carbon across a single year, 774 into 775. That jump has since turned up in tree rings, coral and ice from around the world. The cause is a burst of particles from the Sun roughly ten times past the 1859 Carrington storm, which is the biggest event our instruments have ever seen.

Mind's eye

If the 1859 storm was the king tide that put water over the road, these are the ones that put it through the house. And we have only been keeping the tide book for 170 years.

Two

Stars like ours flare, about once a century.

In December 2024 a team led by Valeriy Vasilyev published in Science. They went through Kepler telescope data on 56,450 stars with the Sun's temperature and behaviour, and found 2,889 superflares on 2,527 of them. The rate works out at roughly one per star per century, which is ten to a hundred times more often than astronomy had estimated.

The paper's own words: if the Sun behaves like the stars in the sample, it could produce superflares at a similar rate.

This is the single most useful fact in the whole argument, and it did not come from the frontier. It came from the mainstream correcting itself, in the most conventional venue available, and it removes "the Sun does not do that" from the table. It does not establish a micronova. It raises the ceiling on what the Sun is known to do, and moves it closer.

Three

The shield fails, and comes back.

A geomagnetic excursion is not a flip. The field weakens sharply, the poles wander a long way from where they belong, and then it recovers to the same polarity it had before. Confusing an excursion with a full reversal is the most common mistake in this argument, and both sides make it.

During Laschamps the field dropped to a small fraction of today's strength. It shows up in lava and lake mud on every continent, and it has been reproduced in global field models rather than resting on any single site. The planet's magnetic shield is not a constant. It has failed, more than once, while people were alive to see it.

Four

It is weakening now, and it broke a map.

Every phone, ship and aircraft that finds north uses a model of the Earth's magnetic field, updated on a five-year cycle. In 2019 that model had to be updated early, for the first time ever, because the north magnetic pole had gone somewhere the model did not expect. That is not a small administrative event. It is the navigation system of the world admitting it had been outrun.

Both halves of that table belong in an honest answer. The changes are real and were strange enough to break a working model. The numbers are also smaller than the frontier states, and the pole is slowing rather than speeding up. Anyone arguing this without carrying the second half will get corrected in public by someone with a search engine.

Five

Micronova is a real word.

In April 2022 Nature published Scaringi and colleagues on hours-long thermonuclear bursts confined to the magnetic poles of collapsed stars. The European Southern Observatory announced it as a new kind of stellar explosion, and named it the micronova. The frontier had been using the word for years before that.

So the chronology is real, and it is regularly denied. It is also regularly over-claimed. The 2022 object is a dead star's surface burning fuel stolen from a companion, and none of that physics carries across to a living star like ours. Sharing a name is not sharing a mechanism.

What the floor buys

And what it does not.

It buys

That the Sun has a much bigger tail than our instruments have seen. That the magnetic shield fails periodically. That it is weakening now. And that the argument about the word is settled in the frontier's favour, as a matter of who said it first.

It does not buy

A 12,000-year clock. A trigger from outside the Solar System. The crust coming loose. A shell thrown off the Sun. Those sit on the upper layers and have to be argued there, on their own evidence, which is thinner.

Anyone who says the floor is empty has not looked. Anyone who treats the floor as the whole building is not being careful either.
