The hand on the scale

What we are doing to it ourselves

A fair question sits behind a lot of the noise on this subject: how much of the near-Earth environment is still natural? The honest answer is that one piece of it has measurably moved because of us, that it is not the piece people usually name, and that the rest of the claims in this area run from documented to nowhere.

One

We moved a boundary in space, with submarine radio.

Very low frequency transmitters broadcast at enormous power so the signal can reach submarines under water. It also goes the other way. It leaks past the atmosphere and wraps the planet in a bubble of radio.

Spacecraft measured where that bubble ends, and found it sitting almost exactly at the inner edge of the radiation belts. Compare against satellite records from the 1960s, when far less of it was being transmitted, and that inner edge has moved noticeably outward. The researcher who named the effect thinks the belts would sit closer in without us.

Which is a strange sentence to be able to write

Human radio has pushed back a structure in near-Earth space, measurably, and it was reported by a NASA mission rather than by anybody with a case to make. There are now proposals to use it deliberately, transmitting to clear excess charged particles out of the belts after a big storm.

And the limit on it

This is an effect on where the radiation belts begin. It is not an effect on how far the aurora reaches, and nobody has connected the two. Reaching for it to explain low-latitude aurora is reaching past what has been shown, when there is already a better explanation sitting there in the weakening of the field.

Known

Two

The heaters, and what they can and cannot do.

Facilities that deliberately warm a patch of the ionosphere with radio are real and there are several, in several countries, which is the part usually treated as conspiracy.

Alaska has one, Norway has one, Russia has one, Puerto Rico had one until the dish collapsed, and China has built a large one. The reasoning that if one military is doing it the others will be too is sound, and in this case it is simply what happened. They exist, they are funded, and the research is published.

The scale is the problem for the bigger claim

What they do is heat a region of the upper atmosphere, hundreds of kilometres up, for minutes at a time, and watch what happens. A hurricane is a heat engine holding an amount of energy that dwarfs anything these can deliver by an enormous factor. There is no published capability to steer one, and the energy accounting is not close.

On the specific claim of a microwave installation in Kamchatka aimed at directing hurricanes: no source for it surfaced in the public record. That is a statement about the public record and not about reality, and the distinction matters more here than almost anywhere, because military research installations are precisely the category that does not appear in searchable literature. Absence of a findable source is not absence of a facility. What it does mean is that the claim cannot be used in an argument, because the person on the other side will look for it and find nothing. Hold it privately if you like; do not lead with it.

The cumulative question is better, and it is genuinely open. Tens of thousands of satellites, an enormous and rising volume of radio in specific bands, and a stream of spacecraft burning up on re-entry and leaving metal in the upper atmosphere that was not there before. Each of those is measured. Whether they add up to something is a real question and it does not yet have a settled answer.

Three

The antipode, checked, and it lands somewhere better.

A geometric claim, and those are the cheapest thing in the world to check and the most expensive thing to get wrong.

PointWhere it isWhat sits opposite
South Atlantic Anomaly, centreAbout 26 to 30 degrees south, 45 to 49 degrees west, near ParaguayAbout 27 to 30 degrees north, 131 to 135 degrees east
That antipode, in wordsThe East China Sea, around the Ryukyu Islands, south of Japan. Not Indonesia, which sits about five degrees south and around 105 to 110 east, a long way off.

And here is the interesting part

That stretch of sea south of Japan is the one people call the Devil's Sea. It has been claimed for decades as the opposite point to the Bermuda Triangle, and that claim is wrong, because Bermuda's antipode is off Western Australia near Perth. But it is roughly the antipode of the South Atlantic Anomaly, which is a real and enormous feature of the Earth's magnetic field rather than a folklore triangle.

So the geometry that failed was attached to the wrong anomaly. Drop the Bermuda pairing, keep the antipodal instinct, and point it at the thing that is actually measured.

Worth keeping separate from a different claim it gets merged with. The frontier material has the two magnetic poles tracking toward a meeting place between Sumatra and Java, and notes that the Indonesian government built a magnetic observatory in that region. That is about where the poles are heading, not about what sits opposite the South Atlantic. Two different things, and running them together is how a checkable claim gets muddied.

Four

Tesla, sorted into three piles.

His name gets attached to everything in this territory, which does him no favours, because one third of it is real and remarkable and gets buried under the other two.

The death ray

announced 1934
  • Status → announced, never built, funding refused
    Unsupported A particle-beam concept he publicised in his late seventies and tried without success to sell, including to the younger Morgan. No device was ever made or demonstrated. When he died in 1943 the government took his papers, and the engineer sent to assess them reported that the last fifteen years of his thinking were speculative, philosophical and somewhat promotional, with no new workable principles for a weapon of that kind. And that assessment is worth far less than it is usually given credit for. It was carried out by the same government that had just seized the papers, and a finding of nothing here is exactly what you would expect to be published whether or not there was something. It is not an independent evaluation and it should not be quoted as one. And the fallback this page retreated to, that no device was ever built or demonstrated, is not solid either. It is the same reported record from the same interested parties, which is precisely the thing just called unreliable. Not built is a claim about what was disclosed. There is a second problem with the way the death ray gets waved away, which is that the category stopped being exotic a long time ago. Directed energy is fielded hardware. A 150 kW laser flies on USS Portland, a 60 kW dazzler-and-laser system on USS Preble, eight more destroyers carry optical dazzlers, laser-armed destroyers were in active theatres as of March 2026, and the US Navy's Project METEOR is putting a high-power microwave weapon to sea this year. Wireless power transfer sits on kitchen benches. Radar is high-power directed radio. A domestic microwave oven is a directed emitter that cooks tissue, sold in every country on Earth. So the honest position is narrow: what was speculative in 1934 was one particular device and one particular set of performance claims, not the idea of concentrated energy at a distance. Treating the whole category as fantasy, from inside a world full of working examples, is the sillier error of the two.

The earthquake machine

the 1898 Houston Street story
  • Status → real patents, a real principle, and one anecdote from the man himself decades later
    Disputed The mechanical oscillator is real and patented. The story is that a version about seven inches long and a pound or two in weight, small enough for a coat pocket, shook his building until the neighbours called the police, and that he took a sledgehammer to it. That account comes from Tesla in later life, not from anyone else at the time. The underlying principle is not nonsense: resonance genuinely does let a small repeated push build a large motion, which is why soldiers break step on bridges. What kills the big version is damping. Ground and buildings soak up energy enormously, and the amount needed climbs far faster than a pocket device can supply.

Colorado Springs, and the thing he was actually right about

1899
  • Status → he was pointing at a real object with instruments that could not resolve it
    Known Disputed He drove very high voltages into the ground and concluded that the Earth would ring, that the whole planet and the air above it formed a cavity with a note of its own. He put that note at around eight cycles a second. Half a century later the physics was worked out properly and the Earth-ionosphere cavity does resonate, with its lowest mode near 7.8 cycles a second. It carries somebody else's name. Held honestly, because the popular version overstates it: his equipment was not capable of resolving those modes, and there is no written record of him measuring them. He identified the right physical object and reasoned to roughly the right number without the means to confirm it. That is a smaller claim than the one usually made for him and it is still an extraordinary piece of intuition.

Five

On seeing weld marks in the landscape.

Someone who has run a MIG torch, a TIG torch, oxy and spot for years knows what an electrical discharge does to material in a way that reading about it does not give you. That is a real basis for noticing a shape, and it should not be waved off.

It is also the weakest form of evidence there is, for a reason that has nothing to do with who is looking. Shapes are cheap. Branching, cratering, glassing, rilles and scarring all arise from many processes, and the eye that knows one of them well will find it everywhere. The plasma-physics reading of rock art runs into exactly this, and the field has a name for the trap.

The way to turn it into something

Stop arguing about the shape and go after the material. A large electrical discharge does not only carve, it leaves evidence of itself: melt textures and glass where nothing was hot enough to melt, magnetic direction frozen into the rock at odds with the field around it, particular elements moved or concentrated, mineral phases that only form under those conditions. Those are laboratory questions with yes or no answers, and they can be asked of a specific outcrop by anyone who can get a sample to a lab.

Which is a better use of the observation than another photograph. If the marks are what they look like, the rock will say so under a microscope, and if it does not, that is worth knowing too.

The documented case, and it needs no speculation

A programme on citizens, then the files burned on purpose.

Elsewhere this site says that absence from the public record is not absence. That rule is usually argued about in the abstract. There is no need: there is a case where the record was deliberately destroyed, and it is admitted.

MKULTRA ran from the early 1950s. Drugs, hypnosis and psychological technique tested on people, including people who had not consented and did not know. That is not contested and has not been for fifty years. What matters most for the argument here is what happened to the paperwork: in 1973 the Director of Central Intelligence ordered the files destroyed, and around 152 boxes went. What survived and later surfaced did so largely by accident, through a misfiled set of financial records that escaped the shredder.

Which turns an abstract rule into a worked example

A researcher in 1975 searching the public record for evidence of a mind-control programme would have found almost nothing, and would have been entirely correct to report that they found almost nothing. They would also have been wrong about the world.

That is the whole of the rule. "No source found" describes the searchable record. It does not describe reality, and where an institution has demonstrably destroyed its own record, the two are known to differ. It forbids treating the absence of a document as the settlement of a question.

Known

The subject was reopened in 2026. A House task force on declassification held a hearing on 30 June titled Mind Control and Accountability, examining why the material stayed classified for decades and whether the agency has met its obligations since, with the 1973 destruction squarely in view. A former officer testified that the temptation to run such a programme remains. A separate witness testified that around forty boxes of sensitive material, including MKULTRA and assassination records, were removed from an intelligence office during a declassification effort.

And here the distinction has to be held hard, or everything above is wasted

Sworn testimony at a hearing is evidence that a person said something, under penalty for lying. It is not evidence that the content is true. The hearing happening is a fact. The claims made inside it are claims, and some of what has been said at these hearings goes a very long way past what any document supports.

Worth knowing that the sharpest published criticism of the task force's direction has come from the National Security Archive, which is the organisation that has spent decades prying this material out of agencies under freedom of information law. Their argument is that the effort should stay on real and recoverable secrets rather than drift into unfalsifiable ones. That is not the accused institution defending itself. It is the side that wants the files open, warning that the wrong questions will bury the right ones, and it deserves more weight than either an official denial or a dramatic allegation.

The same shape applies to the anomalous-phenomena question, and the institutional position has moved again. Legislation before the current Congress would require every federal agency to declassify its records on the subject and publish them; the current defence authorisation carries several provisions aimed at the office responsible, including a requirement to brief lawmakers on intercepts by the North American commands going back to 2004; and the declassification task force has held its own hearing on transparency and whistleblower protection. None of that establishes anything about what the objects are. What it establishes is that the earlier official conclusion is not being treated as final by the legislature that commissioned the office, which is a different and much smaller statement, and the right one to make.

An evidence ledger for one contested argument. Not a verdict, and not an endorsement: the corrections run in both directions on purpose.
