Why any of this belongs on a site about the Sun

The stories are survival equipment

There is a way of handling old traditions that treats them as charming and finished, and a way that treats them as literal history, and both throw away the thing that actually matters. A third reading is available and it has a body count attached to it: some of these are instructions, they were built to survive the loss of everything else, and where one has been kept intact it has worked in living memory.

One

Seven people died, out of seventy thousand.

Start here, because everything else on this page depends on whether the mechanism works at all, and this is the case where it was tested under the worst conditions available.

Simeulue is an island off the west coast of Sumatra. On Boxing Day 2004 it sat about sixty kilometres from the epicentre of the earthquake that killed roughly a quarter of a million people around the Indian Ocean, and it was among the first land the wave reached. Aceh, on the mainland, lost something like a hundred and seventy thousand.

Simeulue lost seven, from a population near seventy thousand.

Not because of a warning system, a siren, a text message or a seawall. There were none. What the island had was smong: a word, a poem, and a story that had been told there since the tsunami of 1907. It is kept in bedtime stories, in song, in commemorations, and cut into grave markers. It carries one instruction, and the instruction is operational rather than moral.

What the story actually says

If the earthquake is strong, and then the sea pulls back, do not go and look. Run for the high ground.

That is the whole payload. It names a trigger, it names the trap, and it names the action. It carried for ninety-seven years across at least three generations with no writing required, no institution maintaining it, and no funding. And on the day, it worked.

Known

This was written up in the earthquake engineering literature afterwards rather than in a folklore journal, which is the correct home for it. It is not a story about the charm of local wisdom. It is a documented case of a non-written information system outperforming every built system in the region on the day it was needed.

Two

Which answers the question about why the old stories are all disasters.

It is a fair thing to notice and it is usually raised as a mystery. It has a plain answer, and the plain answer is more useful than the mysterious one.

People have been people for a very long time, and almost none of what they said has reached us. What did reach us is heavily weighted towards floods, fire from the sky, the long dark, the ground opening, the people who went under the hill. Ask why that particular subject matter survived when everything else was lost, and Simeulue supplies the mechanism.

The stories that carried instructions had descendants to carry them

A tradition that told you to run for the ridge was retold by the people who ran for the ridge. One that did not was not retold by anybody, because there was nobody. Over enough cycles, the surviving corpus is not a random sample of what people once said. It is the subset that was load-bearing.

Which is a selection argument rather than a mystical one, and it is stronger for that. It predicts exactly the pattern that gets noticed: disproportionate catastrophe, disproportionate instruction, and an emphasis on signs, because a sign is the part that has to arrive before the event.

It also predicts something the dismissive reading cannot account for: the durability is the whole design. Song, rhyme, ceremony, taboo, initiation, repetition to children. Those are not decoration on the message. They are error correction, and they are what allows a payload to cross centuries of illiterate transmission with the operational part intact. A civilisation that expects to keep its libraries does not build like that. One that has watched libraries go does.

Three

The whole corpus, sorted by what is actually known about it.

The usual argument runs everything together and then wins or loses on the weakest item. Sorted properly, the material behaves very differently at different tiers, and the sort itself is the useful part.

The point of sorting it this way

Almost every public fight over this material happens at the third tier, which is where it is weakest and where nobody can win. The case is made at the first tier and it is already made. Narrative demonstrably carries actionable, accurate, dated information across many generations without writing. That is established, published, and has saved a documented island.

Once the mechanism is proven, a tradition at the second tier is not a fairy tale that needs defending. It is a signal with an unidentified source, which is a completely different object, and the correct response to it is to go looking for the source rather than to rule on the story.

Four

And the underground one is not a tradition at all. You can walk into it.

Cities under the ground turn up in the corpus everywhere, and get filed with the dragons. They should not be, because at least one is open to visitors.

At Derinkuyu in Cappadocia there is a city cut down into soft rock across eighteen levels, reaching about eighty-five metres below the surface, with an estimated capacity of up to twenty thousand people. It has homes, stables, cellars, wells, and more than fifty ventilation shafts driven up to the surface. It was found in 1963 when a man knocked through a wall in his basement.

Read what it was built for, because the architecture states it plainly

Wells that cannot be poisoned from above. Ventilation that cannot be found or blocked easily. Stone doors that seal from the inside only. Livestock stabled on the upper levels and people below. Storage for months.

This is refuge architecture, and it is enormous. It was used as one, by Christian communities sheltering from persecution and later from raiding. Whatever the first builders were escaping, nobody excavates eighty-five metres of rock and fifty air shafts as a hobby. Somebody expected to need to disappear underground with a whole population, and to stay there.

Known

Which is worth holding next to the second-tier traditions of going under the hill and waiting for the sky to finish. It does not confirm any of them. It does establish that the described behaviour is real, achievable at scale with hand tools, and was carried out, which removes the objection that the stories describe something nobody would or could do.

Five

What to do with the rest of it.

Third-tier material is not going to be settled by argument, and dismissing it has no more evidence behind it than accepting it. What can be done is to say, for each, what would actually move it.

A tradition with no confirmed referent has not been refuted. It is unresolved, and those are different words describing different situations. The useful question is never whether to believe it. It is what finding would change its tier, because a claim with no such finding attached is not doing any work.

The claimWhat would move it
Dragons and giants everywherePartly moved already, in a direction few expect. There is serious scholarship arguing that fossil beds drove the griffin and dragon traditions of the ancient world, with the geography of the traditions tracking the geography of the bone beds. That is a testable claim about where stories cluster, and more of it can be done.
Sky ships, sky palaces, flying craftDating and translation of the source texts, done adversarially and published, rather than quoted through a chain of secondary retellings. Most of the argument currently runs on translations nobody in it has checked.
Sunken continentsBathymetry and crustal composition, which have already answered it for the specific proposed locations. Continental crust is buoyant and does not vanish; it leaves an unmistakable signature. Sunken land is a different and confirmed claim, and the coastlines flooded at the end of the last glacial are real, mapped, and enormous.
Old gods as visitorsPhysical evidence of a kind that admits no other reading, which is a high bar precisely because iconography and text admit many readings. Note the standing correction: the field does not hold that Earth is a fluke, so this argument has fewer opponents than it thinks.
Possession and parasitismWorth stating carefully because the mechanism class is real and well documented: parasites that alter host behaviour in specific, directed ways exist and are studied, from the fungus that drives ants to a chosen height before killing them, to the fluke that steers them up a grass blade to be eaten. Behaviour-altering infection is not exotic biology. Applying it to any particular tradition is speculation and should be labelled as such, but the objection that no such mechanism exists is simply wrong.
Disputed Speculative

One rule applies across the fourth tier and it is not a scientific one. Living traditions belong to the people who hold them. They are not raw material for someone else's theory, they are not evidence to be extracted, and reading them as garbled memories of something else is a way of telling their holders they do not understand their own account. The correct posture toward material in that tier is to leave it with its people unless invited, and the correct posture toward everything above it is to go and look for the referent.

Six

Who was actually keeping it.

Worth finishing on, because the smong case is not only evidence about information transmission. It is evidence about who does the transmitting, and it is not who you would expect from the way the subject is usually discussed.

No institution maintained that poem. No department was funded to hold it, no ministry audited it, and nobody's career depended on getting it right. It was held by people telling their children a rhyme at bedtime, for ninety-seven years, through occupation, poverty and a long civil conflict, by a population with no particular reason to expect anyone would ever thank them for it.

And that is the honest shape of the problem

The record gets lost in two directions at once. Some of it is destroyed deliberately, by institutions with something to protect, and this site has the documented case of that: an admitted programme whose files were burned on a director's order, leaving a researcher who searched honestly in 1975 with nothing to find and no way to know why. And some of it is lost through simple neglect, when the people holding something true have no power, no publisher and no funding.

Both kinds of loss look identical from the outside. An empty archive tells you nothing about which one happened, which is why an absent document settles nothing, and why going and looking beats ruling from the desk.

The last thing worth saying is the plainest. Almost nobody keeping these traditions was doing it to win an argument, and almost nobody is now. The overwhelming majority of people, then and now, want a quiet life, enough to go around, work that means something, and their kids safe: peace, and abundance held responsibly enough that it lasts. The stories were kept by those people, for those people, so that some of them would still be here afterwards.

Which is the same reason for this site. Not to be right in a fight. To have the true version written down somewhere findable, in a form that survives, before it is needed. That is what smong was, and it is the only thing on this page that has actually been tested.

Worth ending on what that did not require. Those families were not preparing, stockpiling or waiting for the end. They were living: working, marrying, raising children, telling the children a rhyme at bedtime along with all the other rhymes. The knowledge sat inside an ordinary and reasonably happy life and cost nothing until the morning it saved almost everyone on the island. Read this material as a reason for dread and you get bunkers, which help one household once. Read it as a design constraint and you get local food, local water, spread skills, kept records and neighbours who know each other, all of which make an ordinary decade better and happen to carry a bad one.
