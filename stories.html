The archive nobody counts as an archive

The stories that lasted

The word "myth" does a lot of quiet work. It sorts an account into the bin marked untrue before anyone checks. And on this particular subject the checking has been done, in Australia more thoroughly than anywhere else on Earth, and a good many of them turn out to be records.

One

It is a field, and it has a name.

Geomythology: the study of accounts handed down by word of mouth that turn out to describe real geological events. It was named by a geologist in 1968 and it is ordinary academic work, not a fringe pursuit. The method is simple to state and hard to do. Take the story, take the geology, and see whether they line up in place, in kind, and in time.

Where the three line up, the account is not a myth. It is testimony, from people who were there, arriving by a channel we are not used to reading.

Known

Two

The strongest case on Earth is here.

Around the whole Australian coastline there are accounts held by Aboriginal groups describing a time when the sea came in and took country that had been dry. Not one story. Twenty-one, from twenty-one locations, from every part of the coast. Nineteen were recovered from written collections made over the last two centuries; two came from speaking with people directly.

The dating is not done by assuming that water taking land means old, which would not work, because water takes land in a hurry too and always has. It is done by depth. For each site they worked out the minimum amount of water that would have to be taken away for the account to be literally true, and then read off the established curve of post-glacial sea-level rise the most recent moment the coast could actually have looked that way.

Which is what separates it from a wave

A tsunami arrives and leaves. It does not lower the sea afterwards. These accounts describe country that is now under a particular depth of water and was once walked on, which is a permanent change in where the coast is, not a flood that came and went. That is the discriminator, and it is doing the work that the bare phrase "the sea came in" cannot.

There is a second argument alongside it, and it is the geographic one: the same specific element, the sea coming in over land that people used, turns up at sites scattered right around the continent. Independent invention of the same idea in that many separate places is a harder thing to believe than a lot of people watching the same slow process.

Which makes them dated documents

Published in the Australian Geographer in 2016 by a marine geographer and a linguist working together, and their conclusion is put plainly: the stories provide empirical corroboration of the sea-level rise that followed the last ice age. Not a resonance, not a vague echo. Corroboration. Across something like three hundred generations of people telling other people.

Country that was walked on is under water now, and the account of it going under was kept, accurately, for seven thousand years.

The part that matters most is not that the stories are old. It is that we know how they stayed accurate. Aboriginal storytelling carries a formal apparatus for it: kin-based systems that track who is responsible for which knowledge, and a cross-checking practice across generations where elders hold each other to the telling. That is a deliberate, maintained technology for keeping a record true across time, and it is the reason these cases exist while the orthodox expectation is that oral accounts rarely hold their shape past a thousand years.

Said carefully

These are living traditions belonging to specific peoples, held by them, and referring to them as evidence in someone else's argument is not the same as having any standing in them. No cultural authority is claimed here and none is implied. What is being pointed at is the published work and what it demonstrates about the durability of a record.

Known

Three

Sandals under the ash.

Mount Mazama, in what is now Oregon, collapsed about seven thousand seven hundred years ago and left the hole that became Crater Lake. The Klamath account of it describes a battle between a spirit of the sky and one of the underworld, and within that: red-hot rocks the size of hills thrown through the air, fire pouring over the forests, the mountain coming down, and the hollow slowly filling with rain.

That is a workable description of a caldera-forming eruption written by someone who watched one. And there is a detail that closes it: archaeologists have found human sandals buried underneath the Mazama ash. People were standing there. The account and the ash layer are about the same event, and one of them is footwear.

Known

Four

Three archives, one winter night.

The cleanest case of all, because the answer was checked three ways by people who had never heard of each other.

  • Japan, written. On the night of 26 January 1700 a tsunami came ashore in Japan with no earthquake before it. Nobody had felt anything. It was recorded carefully in local documents, and it puzzled people for nearly three centuries. It is known as the orphan tsunami: a wave with no parent.
  • The Pacific Northwest, spoken. Accounts held along that coast describe floods and shaking and a great fight between Thunderbird and Whale, the sky against the water. They place the event on a winter night.
  • The ground, physical. Drowned stands of trees still standing where the coast dropped, and layers of sand thrown inland by a wave.

All three are the same event

A rupture along the Cascadia subduction zone, somewhere between magnitude 8.7 and 9.2, on the evening of 26 January 1700. Japanese clerks dated it. The oral accounts described it and put it in the right season. The ghost forests measured it. Three completely independent records, two of them written by people who did not know the other coast existed, and one of them kept by mouth for three hundred years.

Known

Four and a half

The objection that the wave got there first.

Worth taking seriously, because it is the sharpest thing anyone can say about the Australian dating and because half of it is right. Great waves are not tied to the end of an ice age. They can arrive in any century, from a landslide, a seafloor volcano, a rock from the sky. So an account of water coming over the land carries no date of its own.

That is correct, and it is the reason the depth method exists rather than the simpler reading. It is also why the individual accounts have to be handled one at a time. At least one in the set reads on the surface like a sudden event, and the authors say so rather than smoothing it over. Some of these may well be describing a wave.

And there is a whole contested literature underneath it

Great sweeping V and U-shaped sand formations, called chevrons, sit near coastlines in Australia, Madagascar, the Bahamas and Egypt. One group of researchers argued through the 2000s that these are the marks of enormous waves thrown up by ocean impacts within the Holocene, and published the Australian ones as candidate evidence. If they are right, very large waves have hit inhabited coasts recently enough to be remembered.

The rebuttal is specific rather than dismissive, which is the useful kind. Published in Geology in 2009 under the title "Chevrons are not mega-tsunami deposits", the argument is geometric: a wave slows as it approaches shore and comes in square to the coastline, so it should leave marks lying parallel to the shore. The Madagascar formations lie at a sharp angle to it, which is what wind does when nothing is steering it. The formations also turn up further inland than a wave should reach. Their conclusion was that the claim cannot survive simple testing.

And the rebuttal has a scale problem of its own

Both of its tests are calibrated against ordinary waves, and that is worth saying out loud. The refraction argument is sound for a tsunami, which is a very long wave that feels the seabed the whole way in and therefore does swing round to face the shore. It is not obviously sound for the two things being proposed. A wall of water hundreds of metres high stops behaving like a wave and starts behaving like a flood front. And an ocean set moving because the ground underneath it shifted is not a wave at all: its direction is set by the shift, not by the shape of the coast.

The distance test has the same trouble. "Too far inland for a tsunami" is measured against run-ups of around thirty metres of elevation, which is roughly the ceiling for an ordinary one. Lituya Bay in 1958 was measured at five hundred and twenty-four metres, in a confined inlet, witnessed. Something at that scale on an open coast travels a very long way past where the criterion sits.

Which leaves a real position rather than a dismissal

For ordinary tsunami the geometry argument holds and the chevrons look to have gone to the wind. For the mechanisms actually being proposed, the tests were not built to measure them, and nobody appears to have redone the assessment with a flood front or a slopping ocean in mind. That is not a defence of the chevron reading. It is a note that the refutation and the claim are not quite talking about the same thing, which is worth knowing before either is quoted as settled.

Separately, Australian palaeotsunami work does not stand or fall on chevrons. Other lines have been argued from boulders moved to heights waves are not supposed to reach and from bedrock sculpting along the New South Wales coast, and those are their own arguments.

Disputed Unsupported

Four and three quarters

The marks on the rock, and the paper nobody cites.

Alongside what people said, there is what people carved. And there is a claim about the carvings that is usually waved off as fringe when it is in fact peer-reviewed, sitting in a real engineering journal, published twice.

A plasma physicist at Los Alamos compared the shapes that high-current electrical discharges make in a laboratory against the shapes cut into rock by people in the Neolithic and early Bronze Age. Eighty-four separate forms, matched between the lab and the stone, and the same forms turning up on rock faces on opposite sides of the world. His reading was that people were drawing what they saw overhead: an aurora far more violent than anything since, of the kind you would get if the solar wind had been one to two orders of magnitude stronger than it is now.

Where it actually sits

Published in the IEEE Transactions on Plasma Science in 2003, with a second part in 2007. That is a real journal with real review. Anyone saying this material has never been through peer review is wrong, and that particular dismissal gets used a lot.

And the two things wrong with it, which are not the ones usually given

It was reviewed by the wrong people. A plasma physics journal will check the plasma physics, and the plasma physics may well be fine. What it will not check is whether the archaeological reading holds, or whether the dating is right, or whether a palaeomagnetist recognises the sky being described. The claim spans three fields and was reviewed by one.

And matching shapes is the weakest kind of evidence there is. Eighty-four categories, set against the entire worldwide corpus of rock art, is an enormous amount of freedom. Human beings are very good at finding the pattern they went looking for, and there is a name for it in this exact field, reinforcement syndrome, which turns up in the excursion literature too.

The way to settle it is not to argue about the drawings. It is to go and look for the sky they imply.

Because there is a testable claim buried in it, and it is a good one. A solar wind a hundred times stronger for any sustained period is not a subtle thing. It would show in the cosmogenic isotopes, in the ice, in the tree rings, in the same archives that carry the Miyake events and the beryllium. Either the signature is there in the right millennia or it is not. As far as can be found, nobody has run that check against this specific claim, from either direction, in twenty-odd years. It sits with the other open tests.

What does not carry over

The bigger version of this, that whole landscapes are discharge scars, that the Grand Canyon or Uluru or the Eye of the Sahara were cut by something electrical, is a different proposition and it is not in the journals. The conventional accounts of those places are detailed, tested, and in the Saharan case an impact origin was seriously proposed and then ruled out on the evidence rather than by assumption. Asking whether erosion is doing too much explanatory work is a fair question to keep asking. Answering it with a landform that has a worked-out history is not the way in.

Five

What this settles, and what it does not.

Settled

That accounts handed down by mouth can carry accurate, specific, dateable information about real events across thousands of years, given a culture built to keep them. Seven thousand years is demonstrated. The word "myth", used to mean "not a record", is simply wrong about these cases, and that is a published finding rather than an opinion.

Not settled

That any particular story is a record. The work is telling which ones are, and it takes matching the account to a real event in the right place, of the right kind, at a time that fits. The Australian cases pass that test. Plenty of others have not been put to it, and some will not survive it. Treating every old story as data is the mirror of treating every old story as fiction, and it is no better.

Which is the useful position. Not that the stories are true because they are old, and not that they are false because they are stories. That they are a form of evidence with its own strengths and its own failure modes, and that it has been shown to reach much further back than the people dismissing it assumed.
