The part usually called the physics nightmare

The Earth turning over

Of everything in the catastrophist case, this is the piece that gets laughed at fastest and the piece with the most respectable mainstream cousin. There is a published, accepted name for a planet rolling over relative to its own spin axis, it is not crustal displacement, and it is worth knowing which of the two you are arguing for before anyone asks.

One

It has a name, and it is not the one you think.

Two different claims get bundled together and only one of them survives contact with a geophysicist. Separating them is the single most useful thing on this page.

True polar wander

The whole solid Earth, mantle and crust together, rolls relative to the spin axis. The planet keeps spinning the same way in space; the rock underneath rotates around it, so the geographic poles end up over different ground. It happens because moving enough mass around changes what the planet finds easiest to spin about, and the planet obliges.

Accepted. Documented in the magnetic record, actively published in 2025.

Crustal displacement

The crust alone slips over the mantle beneath it, like skin sliding on an orange, while the interior keeps its orientation. This is the Hapgood and Chan Thomas version, and the one the frontier material builds on.

Not accepted. The sticking point is the grip between the crust and what is under it, which is the same problem Einstein could not solve for Hapgood.

Both produce the same headline: ground that was tropical ends up polar. If what you care about is the observable, the accepted mechanism already gives it to you, and you do not have to defend the contested one to have it.

Known Speculative

Two

Somebody did publish ninety degrees.

Not a forum post. Science, 1997, Kirschvink and colleagues, arguing that in the early Cambrian the Earth's entire mantle and crust rolled about ninety degrees relative to the spin axis, because the way mass was distributed had become unstable.

The whole surface of the planet turning through a right angle in around fifteen million years, at roughly ten times the usual speed of continental drift.

It even came with a proposed consequence: that the upheaval had something to do with the Cambrian explosion, the moment complex animal life suddenly diversified. So the idea that a reorientation of the planet reshapes what lives on it is not a fringe framing either.

And it did not hold

The evidence rests on which ancient magnetic directions you accept, and that selection has been argued over hard ever since. Re-analysis of the original data, new measurements from other continents and a revised timescale point the other way, and the ninety-degree version is not currently supported. What survived is the smaller version: real polar wander, better documented, including a shift around eighty-four million years ago, and a 2025 study characterising the movement over the last three hundred and twenty million years as slow.

Which is the honest shape of it. The planet does roll. It is not established that it has ever rolled fast, and every well-supported case takes millions of years rather than a day.

Known Disputed

Three

Antarctica was warm, and we have known since 1912.

On the way back from the South Pole in January 1912, dying, hauling a sledge, Scott's party stopped below Mount Buckley to get out of the wind and picked up sixteen kilograms of rock. They kept it. They were still dragging it when they were found.

In the rock were fossil leaves and coal. Scott wrote of a piece of coal with leaves traced through it in layers, and stems well enough preserved to show their cell structure. The plant is Glossopteris, a seed fern that grew across the southern supercontinent, and the same leaves turn up in the Permian coal measures of India. Two hundred and eighty million years old, and the beds nearby carry fossil tree trunks and root systems.

So the observable is not in dispute at all

Antarctica was temperate and forested. Ground that is now under kilometres of ice grew trees. Those fossils became some of the strongest early evidence for continental drift, because the same plant sat on continents that are now oceans apart.

The mechanism is the ordinary one: the continent moved, over a hundred million years and more, as part of the slow break-up of Gondwana. Not a flip. But anyone told that the poles have always been the poles can be handed a dying man's rock samples, and that is a better opening than most.

Known

Four

Glaciers at the equator, which is two questions.

Worth splitting, because one half is not mysterious and the other half is one of the longest-running unresolved arguments in geology.

The ones you can visit are just high up. Cayambe in Ecuador carries the only snow anywhere the equator actually crosses, and it does so at about four and a half thousand metres. Kilimanjaro, Mount Kenya, the Rwenzori, Puncak Jaya in Papua. Altitude, not latitude, and all of them are shrinking fast. Over ninety-nine per cent of tropical ice is in the Andes. Nothing here needs explaining beyond cold air being thin and high.

The ones in the rock are a different matter entirely. Around seven hundred million years ago, glaciers left their marks on ground that the magnetic evidence puts close to the equator at the time. Ice at sea level, in the tropics. That has been a paradox since the 1960s and it has produced exactly two serious answers.

Snowball Earth

The whole planet froze, oceans and all, right to the tropics, and then came out of it. Named in 1992, and the mainstream front-runner.

The Earth was lying on its side

If the tilt of the axis exceeded about fifty-four degrees, the equator receives less sunlight over a year than the poles do. Turn the planet far enough and the cold place is the middle. Ice at the equator, warm poles, and ferocious seasons everywhere.

The second one is published, and it is not new

George Williams has argued it for decades, in Nature and in the review literature, formalised as a named hypothesis: high tilt, low-latitude ice, strong seasonality. It exists precisely because glaciers at the equator are hard to explain any other way. So a mainstream geologist proposing that the Earth's whole orientation was radically different, to account for exactly the evidence you are pointing at, is a real thing that happened in real journals.

The trouble with it is specific and worth carrying: the mechanism proposed for getting the tilt back down again has been shown not to work. So it is a live minority position with a known hole in it, rather than a winner. That is still a great deal more than most people expect to find.

One objection to retire, though

The usual next line is that the Moon holds the tilt steady, so a high tilt is impossible. That is true of the present arrangement and it is not a law. The steadying depends on how far away the Moon is and how fast the Earth turns, both of which have changed a great deal, and the published estimate is that tidal drift eventually takes the protection away entirely. More to the point, one of the leading models of how the Moon got here has the Earth tipped about seventy degrees over at the time. Worth its own page.

Known Disputed

Five

Where the proposed mechanism runs out.

The version where a solar blast charges up the equator and the planet reorients around it is the step that has nothing behind it, and saying so is what keeps the rest of this page usable.

Reorienting a planet is a question about where its mass sits. Polar wander works because shifting enough weight changes what the Earth finds easiest to spin about, and the planet rolls until the heavy part reaches the equator. That is a mass argument from beginning to end. Electric charge does not move mass around on that scale, and there is no worked mechanism connecting a charged upper atmosphere to the distribution of a planet's interior. Nobody has published one, including the people making the claim.

Which leaves the useful position

The observable is real and does not need the mechanism. Ground that was tropical is now polar, established from fossils. The solid Earth does roll relative to its spin axis, established from magnetism. Whether either can happen quickly is genuinely open. Whether a solar event can cause it is not open, it is simply unsupported, and leading with it costs you the two claims that hold.

Six

And the one to drop.

The Bermuda Triangle is the weakest card in this hand, and the antipode version of it is wrong in a way anyone can check in about ten seconds.

The claimWhat is actually the case
Ships and aircraft vanish there at an unusual rateThe US ocean and atmosphere agency finds no evidence that disappearances happen more often there than in any other large, busy stretch of ocean.
It is one of two places where compasses point to true northHalf true and not an anomaly. A line of zero declination has run through the region, and on such a line a compass happens to agree with the map. That is the absence of a correction, not the presence of a disturbance, and the line drifts.
There is a major magnetic anomaly thereNo evidence of one.
The Devil's Sea off Japan is its antipodeGeometrically false. The point on the far side of the world from Bermuda is in the Indian Ocean off Western Australia, near Perth. Japan is nowhere near it.

The real large magnetic anomalies are elsewhere, and they are mapped, which is not the same as solved. The biggest on Earth is at Kursk in Russia, where the field runs about ten thousand nanotesla off, and the reason is an extraordinary amount of iron ore in the crust underneath. Second is Bangui in the Central African Republic, an ellipse roughly seven hundred by a thousand kilometres. Its cause has been argued over for decades: a large meteorite strike around a billion years ago was proposed in 1992, others have suggested a major fracturing of the crust or a great body of igneous rock, and recent work images banded iron and dense rock down to about thirty-eight kilometres and argues against the impact. That is a working answer with a live dissent attached, not a closed case, and neither anomaly is where the story wants them to be anyway.

Why this matters more than it looks

An argument is priced by its weakest quoted claim. Bring the antipode line into a conversation and the person across from you checks it, finds it wrong in seconds, and now has a reason to stop listening before the fossil forests and the published ninety-degree paper ever come up. Dropping it costs nothing, because it was never carrying anything.

Seven

The test has already been run, and it is in the ice.

There is one dataset that settles whether any of this happened recently, and it is the same dataset the rest of the case leans on. It cuts hard the other way, and saying so here is the price of everything else on these pages being worth reading.

Ice sheets keep layers. Snow falls, gets buried, compresses, and stays put in order, carrying the air and the dust and the volcanic ash of the year it fell. Read the layers and you get a continuous account. Melt the sheet and you get nothing, because the account is the ice.

RecordHow far back, continuous
Antarctica, Dome C800,000 years, eight full climate cycles, undisturbed
Antarctica, the 2025 core to bedrockAt least 1.2 million years, an uninterrupted sequence of cycles, the oldest continuous ice ever recovered
Greenland, north123,000 years, undisturbed, into the last warm period
Greenland, north-westContinuous through the last interglacial, ice as old as 128,000 years

Which forecloses the recent version

A continent that travelled to the tropics twelve thousand or seventy-four thousand years ago would have lost its ice, and with it the record. Instead there is a layered, unbroken sequence running back more than a million years at one pole and past the last warm period at the other. Both ice sheets sat still and stayed cold straight through every window in which a recent crustal shift is claimed. Antarctica reached its present polar position around eighty-three million years ago and has carried ice since about thirty-four million.

The ice is the strongest evidence for the recurrence argument and the strongest evidence against a recent turn. Same cores, both ways.

It is worth being exact about what this closes and what it leaves open. It does not touch the magnetic excursions, which change the field without moving any ground. It does not touch true polar wander in deep time, well before the ice. And the strongest reply available is a fair one: a smaller shift that left both ice sheets in the cold would not destroy the record. That reply has its own follow-up, though, because the chemistry locked into the ice tracks temperature, so even a modest change of latitude leaves a signature, and the curves for the last million years look like ordinary glacial cycles rather than a continent changing address.

Known

Eight

And yet Toba is genuinely hard to find.

Which is the other half of an honest look at the same archives, and it is the more interesting half.

Toba, about seventy-four thousand years ago, was one of the largest eruptions of the last two million years. It should be the easiest thing in the world to point at: a colossal sulphur signal in both hemispheres at a known date. Its ash is identified confidently on land and in the sea floor.

In the ice, its exact position is ambiguous

Work across five cores from both poles turns up not one signal but four candidates, labelled T1 through T4, all of them sitting inside the uncertainty on Toba's radiometric date. The largest peak near the right depth was used to estimate a sulphur load somewhere between about 230 and 1,140 million tonnes over roughly six years, which is a range wide enough to drive a truck through, and the question of which peak is actually Toba is still being argued.

So the archive is not the clean ledger it is often presented as. The biggest bang in two million years, with a date from the rock itself, and the ice offers a choice of four. That is a real caution about how confidently anything gets pinned in these records, and it applies to the sceptic's side of every argument on these pages as much as anyone's.
