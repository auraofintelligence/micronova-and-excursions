One argument, sorted into columns

Sorted, not settled

The solar micronova argument almost never gets past its first thirty seconds. It arrives wrapped in things that read as disqualifying, the wrapper gets recognised, and the parcel goes in the bin without being opened. What is inside is a set of physical claims, some of which mainstream science has been quietly moving towards for fifteen years, and some of which do not survive an hour with the literature. Both of those are worth knowing, and neither one is what you get told.

Start with what is settled How to split the question

The first move

It is not one question.

"Is the micronova real?" gets answered as though it were a single yes or no, and that is where the conversation goes wrong. It is at least five questions stacked on each other, and they have different answers. The order below matters: the further down you go, the less support there is. Almost every public argument about this happens on the last one, as though losing it took the first three with it.

  1. L1 Do the Earth's magnetic poles collapse and wander, periodically? Known Yes. Documented on every continent. The last big one was about 41,000 years ago and took the field down by as much as 90 per cent.
  2. L2 Does the Sun do things far bigger than anything we have measured? Known Yes, and this came from mainstream astronomy, twice, in 2012 and again in 2024.
  3. L3 Does the Sun's neighbourhood change, and our place in it? Known Yes, and both halves. A cold dense cloud squeezed the Sun's protective bubble down inside the Earth's orbit two million years ago, and passing stars reshape the planets' own orbits. Both published in 2024.
  4. L4 Can a change like that make a star like ours shed an outer layer? Speculative The genuinely open question, and narrower than it is usually put. Stars shedding discrete shells is photographed rather than hypothetical: Betelgeuse threw off part of its surface in 2019, and detached shells stand off carbon stars on a repeating internal clock. What is missing is a star burning hydrogen in its core doing it.
  5. L5 And does it run on a clean clock, every 6,000 or 12,000 years? Disputed The weakest of the five, and the one the conclusion needs least.
  • Known sourced fact or measured record
  • Modelled a calculation anyone can re-run
  • Disputed live contest, with the point of contest named
  • Speculative physics permits it; nobody has shown it
  • Unsupported looked for, not found

The part nobody argues about

Four things science already grants.

These come from journals, satellites and government agencies, not from anyone on the frontier of this argument. They are the floor. Anyone who says there is no evidence has not stood on it.

10× Roughly how far past the 1859 Carrington storm the biggest confirmed solar particle events sit. They show up as a spike of carbon-14 in a single year of tree rings. Five are well confirmed, the oldest around 7176 BCE.
1 / century How often stars like the Sun let go a superflare, on Kepler telescope data published in Science in December 2024. That is ten to a hundred times more often than astronomy thought before.
~41,000 yrs Since the Laschamps event, when the Earth's magnetic shield fell to a small fraction of today's strength and the poles wandered. It is recorded in lava and mud on every continent.
9% How much weaker the field is than when the first proper measurements were taken in 1840. Roughly one tile in eleven off the roof, and the weak patch over the South Atlantic has grown since 2014 by an area near two thirds of the Australian mainland.

We have been measuring the Sun for about 170 years and drawing conclusions about something with thousand-year rhythms. That is judging the tides from one afternoon on the sand.

The argument that needs no history at all

None of this is exotic. It is ordinary.

The sentence doing most of the work against all of it is that catastrophe is an extraordinary claim. Exoplanet astronomy has quietly made that the wrong category, and none of what follows requires believing anything about Earth's past.

Catastrophic planetary events and their observational status
The eventWhere it stands elsewhere in the sky
A planet thrown out of its systemFree-floating worlds are found by microlensing and directly by telescope, and may outnumber the planets that kept their star below Earth mass. Ejection is a standard outcome of the dynamics.
Endless day on one face, endless night on the otherExpected for terrestrial planets around red dwarfs, which are the commonest star there is. That is arguably the normal planetary condition, and Earth's daily rotation is the odd case.
A star reworking a planet's airModelled and constrained. Flares from an active red dwarf drive ozone up around twentyfold and lift night-side winds by tens of metres per second.
A planet falling into its sunCaught in the act. A survey picked up the optical flash, with dust thrown off as the planet spiralled into the star's outer layers.
Everything eaten at the endBetween a quarter and a half of all white dwarfs carry iron, calcium and magnesium that can only be swallowed planetary material. Visibly consuming your own planetary system is close to the standard end state.

Which moves the burden without asserting anything about here

Every line above is ordinary planetary physics with a paper behind it. So the claim that planets do not have things like that happen to them is simply false, and it was the sentence carrying the argument.

What is unusual is not the catastrophe. It is our own stretch without one, and that is a fact about the length of the record rather than about how the universe behaves.

Why any of it is worth the trouble

Seven people died, out of seventy thousand.

The reasonable objection to all of this is: suppose you are right, what would anyone do about it. There is an answer, it has been tested under the worst conditions available, and it cost nothing.

Simeulue is an island off Sumatra. On Boxing Day 2004 it sat about sixty kilometres from the epicentre of the earthquake that killed roughly a quarter of a million people around the Indian Ocean, and it was among the first land the wave reached. Aceh, on the mainland, lost something like a hundred and seventy thousand.

Simeulue lost seven, from a population near seventy thousand.

No siren, no seawall, no warning system of any kind. What the island had was smong: a word, a poem, a story told there since the tsunami of 1907, kept alive in bedtime stories, in song, and cut into grave markers. It carries one instruction, and it is operational rather than moral.

If the earthquake is strong, and then the sea pulls back, do not go and look. Run for the high ground.

Trigger, trap, action. It carried for ninety-seven years across three generations, with no writing, no institution maintaining it and no funding, and on the day it outperformed every built system in the region.

Known

That is the whole argument for bothering. Not to win a fight about the Sun. To have the true version written down somewhere findable, in a form that survives, before it is needed. Everything else on this site is an attempt to work out what the true version actually is, and to say honestly which parts are not known yet.

Where it lands

The smaller true thing.

After the tests in what actually lands, the mechanism does not survive, the clock does not survive, and the timing does not survive. What does survive is narrower and tougher than either side tends to say out loud.

What holds

The Sun's demonstrated ceiling is far above anything in the instrumental record. The magnetic shield is measurably weakening, faster in some places than the global average. The consequences of those two together are underpriced in public planning. None of that needs a micronova to be true, and all of it comes from the mainstream.

And the part that comes apart

Whether to harden against a long blackout is a separate question from whether the mechanism is right, and most arguments about this run the two together. Spare transformers, local water, local food, a way to keep working with the cable cut: the case for those rests on a Carrington-class storm alone, which is already a planning assumption for governments and insurers. If the deeper claim turns out to be right, the same preparation covers it. If it turns out to be wrong, nothing was wasted.
