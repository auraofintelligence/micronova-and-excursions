Civilisation of Sand

Out of the building, with an hour to spare

Earthquakes kill about 18,300 people a year on the thirty-year average, roughly 350 a week, and more than three quarters of them are killed by a building rather than by the ground. An hour of warning buys one action, and it is the right one: outside, in open ground. Over a century the indoor toll runs to about 1.4 million people, near enough the population of Adelaide. The build under these pages is designed at four sizes, from a household terrace to a sensor network around the Sun, and the evidence here is the reason it is shaped that way.

An hour will not move a city, and moving a city is not what is wanted. People standing outdoors is the whole requirement, and 100 kilometres of accuracy, about an hour on the highway, says which ground. That puts a working warning on the order of a million lives a century, the range depending on compliance rather than physics.

A warning nobody acts on saves nobody, which is why false alarms are the enemy of that number rather than a side issue.

Where the deaths come from

83 per cent of building-collapse deaths happen in countries rated unusually corrupt. Those people are not killed by the earthquake. They are killed by a building code that was written and not enforced. The trigger is geophysical and the toll is a governance failure, which is why the governance half of this work is a separate build.

On the solar side the odds of a direct Carrington-class storm inside a century come out at 65 to 72 per cent on entirely mainstream rates, worse than a coin toss, with the grid, the satellites and the undersea cables as the exposed part. That number waits on nothing argued anywhere on this site.

What is being built

One cell, replicated at four sizes.

Sorted by cause, threats get argued one at a time, each argument winnable by whoever is more patient, so nothing gets built. Sorted by response the arguing becomes optional: eighteen threats come back to six preparations, and one build covers the lot of them.

The six are energy that islands, water that is yours, food and fixing, knowledge that stays, shelter that holds, people who have met before. Volcanic winter, pandemic and grid collapse have the same answer, whatever anyone makes of the rest of these pages.

Inside the 38 cubic metres each part eats another part's waste: fish water to the plants, plant and kitchen waste to the insects and fungi, insects to the birds and fish, birds to the compost. Software runs the conditions, and the crop mix tunes to whatever the household is short of. Cubic metres rather than floor area is on purpose, so the module drops into a flat, an underground block or a hull without a redesign. The terrace version is small and available now: the first instance of the cell rather than a gesture at the city.

Underground, moving water is the heat sink that makes a sealed volume liveable, and it is the water supply and the shielding in the same stroke, which is why the siting is sand and aquifer. The excavation pays for itself, since mineral sands come out as product and part of the remaining silica goes back in as liner. Minjerribah is about 275 square kilometres, so cut to 100 metres that is roughly 27.5 billion cubic metres, near enough 55 Sydney Harbours, and twenty million people take about a third of it.

Across thirty-odd levels that is suburban density per level. The number only sounds impossible in two dimensions.

Four satellites is not an arbitrary floor either. It is the tetrahedron, the smallest number of points that resolves a gradient across three dimensions, which is why ESA flies four Cluster spacecraft and NASA flies four MMS. Parker Solar Probe is one point sampling a plasma that is structured in space and changing in time, and one point cannot separate those: when the instruments record a change, nothing in the data says whether the probe flew through a structure or the structure changed around it. That is geometry rather than budget, and nobody has flown multi-point inside the corona.

Hypotheses go through one pipeline, mainstream and contested alike: state it, name the parameters, implement the model, run it, compare against observation. Three calculations sit in this repo's tools folder, and two came back partly against the thing they were built for. The design work is student work: the author helped open a student innovation lab in Kolhapur, Maharashtra in 2019 alongside a former ISRO mission director, a former DRDO scientist and a nuclear physicist, and it took 200 students in its first year across 12 projects in aerospace, space robotics and automation. India has the largest 5-to-24 population on Earth. Given AI tools, swarm design is coursework rather than a contract.

The pattern underneath all of it

Define the unit cell. Make it owned by whoever is inside it. Replicate. 38 cubic metres for a household, 100 per person underground, four satellites for a picket, one card per project on the island ledger. The same move at every scale is what lets separate ambitions share one stack.

Why the build is shaped this way

Four things science already grants.

These come from journals, satellites and government agencies, not from anyone on the frontier. They are the floor under the shelter and the spare hardware.

10× Roughly how far past the 1859 Carrington storm the biggest confirmed solar particle events sit. They show up as a spike of carbon-14 in a single year of tree rings, five of them well confirmed, the oldest around 7176 BCE.
1 / century How often stars like the Sun let go a superflare, on Kepler data published in Science in December 2024. Ten to a hundred times more often than astronomy thought before.
~41,000 yrs Since the Laschamps event, when the Earth's magnetic shield fell to a small fraction of today's strength and the poles wandered. It is recorded in lava and mud on every continent.
9% How much weaker the field is than when the first proper measurements were taken in 1840. Roughly one tile in eleven off the roof, and the weak patch over the South Atlantic has grown since 2014 by an area near two thirds of Australia.

We have been measuring the Sun for about 170 years and drawing conclusions about something with thousand-year rhythms. That is judging the tides from one afternoon on the sand.

None of this is exotic. It is ordinary.

How to read the rest

It is not one question.

The claim in one line: the Solar System crosses a dusty magnetic boundary in the galaxy, the crossing loads and disturbs the Sun, and the Sun sheds an outer layer. "Is the micronova real?" then gets answered as a single yes or no. It is at least five questions stacked on each other with different answers, so the pages behind this one are sorted that way. The further down, the less support there is, and most public argument happens on the last rung, as though losing it took the first three down with it.

  1. L1 Do the Earth's magnetic poles collapse and wander? Known Yes, documented on every continent. The last big one was about 41,000 years ago and took the field down by as much as 90 per cent.
  2. L2 Does the Sun do things far bigger than anything we have measured? Known Yes, and this came from mainstream astronomy, twice, in 2012 and again in 2024.
  3. L3 Does the Sun's neighbourhood change, and our place in it? Known Modelled Yes, both halves, both published in 2024. A cold dense cloud squeezed the Sun's protective bubble down inside the Earth's orbit two million years ago, and passing stars reshape the planets' orbits.
  4. L4 Can a change like that make a star like ours shed an outer layer? Speculative The genuinely open question, and narrower than it is usually put. Shell-shedding is photographed rather than hypothetical: Betelgeuse threw off part of its surface in 2019, and detached shells stand off carbon stars on a repeating internal clock. What is missing is a star burning hydrogen in its core doing it.
  5. L5 And does it run on a clean clock, every 6,000 or 12,000 years? Disputed The weakest of the five, and the one the conclusion needs least.
  • Known sourced fact or measured record
  • Modelled a calculation anyone can re-run
  • Disputed live contest, with the point of contest named
  • Speculative physics permits it; nobody has shown it
  • Unsupported looked for, not found

Knowledge that stays

The cheapest of the six.

Of the six preparations, the cheapest is the one that carries an instruction across generations with no hardware and no budget behind it. It has been tested under the worst conditions available.

Simeulue is an island off Sumatra. On Boxing Day 2004 it sat about sixty kilometres from the epicentre of the earthquake that killed roughly a quarter of a million people around the Indian Ocean, and it was among the first land the wave reached. Aceh, on the mainland, lost about a hundred and seventy thousand people.

Simeulue lost seven, from a population near seventy thousand.

No siren, no seawall, no warning system of any kind. What the island had was smong: a word, a poem, a story told there since the tsunami of 1907, kept alive in bedtime stories, in song, and cut into grave markers. It carries one instruction, operational rather than moral.

Trigger, trap, action. It carried ninety-seven years across three generations with no writing, no institution and no funding, and on the day it outperformed every built system in the region.

Known

That is the upper bound on the compliance figure at the top of this page, reached with no technology, because the warning was trusted and the action was known. The deliberate version of the trick is being attempted here as a body of songs and the pipeline that films them. A tune is still the cheapest storage there is.

Where it lands

The smaller true thing.

After the tests in what actually lands, the mechanism does not survive, the clock does not survive, and the timing does not survive. What does survive is narrower and tougher than either side tends to say out loud.

What holds

The Sun's demonstrated ceiling is far above anything in the instrumental record. The magnetic shield is measurably weakening, faster in some places than the global average. The consequences of the two together are underpriced in public planning. None of that needs a micronova, and all of it is mainstream.

And the part that comes apart

Whether to build is a separate question from whether the mechanism is right, and most arguments run the two together. The four scales rest on the coin toss and the earthquake arithmetic. If the deeper claim is right, the same build covers it. If it is wrong, nothing was wasted.

And the register this is usually read in is the wrong one

Material like this gets filed under dread, and dread produces bunkers and a kind of joyless waiting. A bunker helps one household for one event and nobody at all the rest of the time. Simeulue is the other model: ordinary lives, and a rhyme that cost nothing until the morning it saved almost everyone.

The six preparations up the page are also the list of things that make an ordinary Tuesday better.

Twelve thousand years of unusual calm produced farming, cities, writing, music, medicine and every good thing anyone has ever made. That is not a countdown, it is a windfall. Windfalls get spent, and some get built into something that outlasts them.

Nobody knows when. Everybody can see it has happened before and will happen again. Between those two facts sits an enormous amount of ordinary, useful work, and almost none of it requires being frightened.
