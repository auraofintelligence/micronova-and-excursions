Civilisation of Sand

Out of the building, with an hour to spare

Earthquakes kill about 18,300 people a year on the thirty-year average, roughly 350 a week, and more than three quarters of them are killed by a building rather than by the ground. An hour of warning buys one action, and it is the right one: outside, standing in open ground. Over a century the indoor toll runs to about 1.4 million people, near enough the population of Adelaide. The build these pages sit under is designed at four sizes, from a household terrace to a sensor network around the Sun. The evidence here is the reason it is shaped that way.

The arithmetic on one hour of warning.

Nothing contested further down this page carries any of the weight below. These are ordinary earthquake mortality figures and ordinary space weather odds, and they hold whether or not a single claim on the rest of the site survives.

An hour will not move a city, and moving a city is not what is wanted. People standing outdoors is the whole requirement, and 100 kilometres of accuracy, about an hour on the highway, is enough to say which ground. That puts a working warning on the order of a million lives a century, with the range depending on compliance rather than on physics.

A warning nobody acts on saves nobody, which is why false alarms are the enemy of that number rather than a side issue.

Where the deaths come from

83 per cent of building-collapse deaths happen in countries rated unusually corrupt. Those people are not killed by the earthquake. They are killed by a building code that was written and not enforced. The trigger is geophysical and the toll is a governance failure, and the governance half of this work sits on a separate site.

On the solar side the same logic runs on figures nobody disputes. The odds of a direct Carrington-class storm inside a century come out at 65 to 72 per cent on entirely mainstream rates, worse than a coin toss, with the grid, the satellites and the undersea cables as the exposed part. That number waits on nothing argued anywhere on this site.

What is being built

One cell, replicated at four sizes.

Sorted by cause, threats get argued one at a time, and each argument is winnable by whoever is more patient, so nothing gets built. Sorted by response, the arguing becomes optional: eighteen threats come back to six preparations, and one build covers the lot of them.

The six are energy that islands, water that is yours, food and fixing, knowledge that stays, shelter that holds, and people who have met before. Somebody who reads every page here as nonsense still owes an answer to volcanic winter, pandemic, crop failure and grid collapse, and it is the same answer.

Inside the 38 cubic metres each part eats another part's waste. Fish water feeds the plants, plant and kitchen waste feeds the insects and the fungi, the insects feed the birds and the fish, the birds feed the compost. Software runs the conditions, and the crop mix can be tuned to whatever the household is short of. It is specified in cubic metres rather than floor area on purpose, so the same module drops into a flat, an underground block or a hull without a redesign. The terrace version is small and available now: the first instance of the cell a city would be made from, rather than a gesture at one.

Underground, moving water is the heat sink that makes a sealed volume liveable, and it is the water supply and the radiation shielding in the same stroke, which is why the siting is sand and aquifer. The excavation pays for itself: mineral sands come out as product, part of the remaining silica goes back in as structural liner, so the spoil is the building, with the surface left to recover above. On one island the arithmetic is checkable. Minjerribah is about 275 square kilometres, so excavated to 100 metres that is roughly 27.5 billion cubic metres, near enough 55 Sydney Harbours. Counting the farms, water, workshops and public space around the dwellings, twenty million people, about four fifths of Australia, come to a third of that, at a depth shallower than Derinkuyu is deep.

Spread over thirty-odd levels, that is suburban density per level. The number only sounds impossible in two dimensions.

The swarm is a picket rather than a mission: four satellites for a low-priority object, 30 to 50 for somewhere like Mars or Titan, 50 to 100 nodes for the Sun. Four is not an arbitrary floor. It is the tetrahedron, the smallest number of points that resolves a gradient across three dimensions, which is why ESA flies four Cluster spacecraft and NASA flies four MMS. Parker Solar Probe is one point sampling a plasma that is structured in space and changing in time, and one point cannot separate those two: when the instruments record a change, nothing in the data says whether the probe flew through a structure or whether the structure changed around it. That is geometry, not budget, and another century of flying would not touch it. Nobody has flown multi-point inside the corona.

Hypotheses go through one pipeline, mainstream and contested alike: state it, name the parameters, implement the model, run it, compare the output against observation. This repo is a worked example of that running by hand. Three calculations sit in the tools folder, built during the arguments on these pages, and two came back partly against the thing they were built for.

The design work is not done by a funded engineering team. It is done by students. The author helped open a student innovation lab in Kolhapur, Maharashtra in 2019, alongside a former ISRO mission director, a former DRDO scientist and a nuclear physicist. It took 200 students in its first year across 12 projects in aerospace, space robotics and automation, with a student rocket launching centre beside it. India has the largest 5-to-24 population on Earth. Given AI tools, swarm design becomes coursework rather than a contract.

The pattern underneath all of it

Define the unit cell. Make it owned by whoever is inside it. Replicate. 38 cubic metres for a household, 100 per person underground, four satellites for a picket, one card per project on the island ledger. It is the same move at every scale, which is what lets separate ambitions share a stack.

Why the build is shaped this way

Four things science already grants.

These come from journals, satellites and government agencies, not from anyone on the frontier of this argument. They are the floor under the shelter and the power and the spare hardware. Anyone who says there is no evidence has not stood on it.

10× Roughly how far past the 1859 Carrington storm the biggest confirmed solar particle events sit. They show up as a spike of carbon-14 in a single year of tree rings. Five are well confirmed, the oldest around 7176 BCE.
1 / century How often stars like the Sun let go a superflare, on Kepler telescope data published in Science in December 2024. That is ten to a hundred times more often than astronomy thought before.
~41,000 yrs Since the Laschamps event, when the Earth's magnetic shield fell to a small fraction of today's strength and the poles wandered. It is recorded in lava and mud on every continent.
9% How much weaker the field is than when the first proper measurements were taken in 1840. Roughly one tile in eleven off the roof, and the weak patch over the South Atlantic has grown since 2014 by an area near two thirds of the Australian mainland.

We have been measuring the Sun for about 170 years and drawing conclusions about something with thousand-year rhythms. That is judging the tides from one afternoon on the sand.

The argument that needs no history at all

None of this is exotic. It is ordinary.

The sentence doing most of the work against all of it is that catastrophe is an extraordinary claim. Exoplanet astronomy has quietly made that the wrong category, and none of what follows requires believing anything about Earth's past.

Catastrophic planetary events and their observational status
The eventWhere it stands elsewhere in the sky
A planet thrown out of its systemFree-floating worlds are found by microlensing and directly by telescope, and may outnumber the planets that kept their star below Earth mass. Ejection is a standard outcome of the dynamics.
Endless day on one face, endless night on the otherExpected for terrestrial planets around red dwarfs, which are the commonest star there is. That is arguably the normal planetary condition, and Earth's daily rotation is the odd case.
A star reworking a planet's airModelled and constrained. Flares from an active red dwarf drive ozone up around twentyfold and lift night-side winds by tens of metres per second.
A planet falling into its sunCaught in the act. A survey picked up the optical flash, with dust thrown off as the planet spiralled into the star's outer layers.

Which moves the burden without asserting anything about here

Every line above is ordinary planetary physics with a paper behind it. So the claim that planets do not have things like that happen to them is simply false, and it was the sentence carrying the argument.

What is unusual is not the catastrophe. It is our own stretch without one, and that is a fact about the length of the record rather than about how the universe behaves.

How to read the rest

It is not one question.

"Is the micronova real?" gets answered as though it were a single yes or no. It is at least five questions stacked on each other and they have different answers, so the pages behind this one are sorted that way. The further down the stack, the less support there is. Most public argument about this happens on the last rung, as though losing it took the first three down with it.

  1. L1 Do the Earth's magnetic poles collapse and wander, periodically? Known Yes. Documented on every continent. The last big one was about 41,000 years ago and took the field down by as much as 90 per cent.
  2. L2 Does the Sun do things far bigger than anything we have measured? Known Yes, and this came from mainstream astronomy, twice, in 2012 and again in 2024.
  3. L3 Does the Sun's neighbourhood change, and our place in it? Known Yes, and both halves. A cold dense cloud squeezed the Sun's protective bubble down inside the Earth's orbit two million years ago, and passing stars reshape the planets' own orbits. Both published in 2024.
  4. L4 Can a change like that make a star like ours shed an outer layer? Speculative The genuinely open question, and narrower than it is usually put. Stars shedding discrete shells is photographed rather than hypothetical: Betelgeuse threw off part of its surface in 2019, and detached shells stand off carbon stars on a repeating internal clock. What is missing is a star burning hydrogen in its core doing it.
  5. L5 And does it run on a clean clock, every 6,000 or 12,000 years? Disputed The weakest of the five, and the one the conclusion needs least.

  • Modelled a calculation anyone can re-run
  • Disputed live contest, with the point of contest named
  • Speculative physics permits it; nobody has shown it
  • Unsupported looked for, not found

Knowledge that stays

Seven people died, out of seventy thousand.

Of the six preparations, the cheapest is the one that carries an instruction across generations with no hardware, no budget and no institution behind it. It has been tested under the worst conditions available.

Simeulue is an island off Sumatra. On Boxing Day 2004 it sat about sixty kilometres from the epicentre of the earthquake that killed roughly a quarter of a million people around the Indian Ocean, and it was among the first land the wave reached. Aceh, on the mainland, lost something like a hundred and seventy thousand.

Simeulue lost seven, from a population near seventy thousand.

No siren, no seawall, no warning system of any kind. What the island had was smong: a word, a poem, a story told there since the tsunami of 1907, kept alive in bedtime stories, in song, and cut into grave markers. It carries one instruction, and it is operational rather than moral.

If the earthquake is strong, and then the sea pulls back, do not go and look. Run for the high ground.

Trigger, trap, action. It carried for ninety-seven years across three generations, with no writing, no institution maintaining it and no funding, and on the day it outperformed every built system in the region.

Where it lands

The smaller true thing.

After the tests in what actually lands, the mechanism does not survive, the clock does not survive, and the timing does not survive. What does survive is narrower and tougher than either side tends to say out loud.

What holds

The Sun's demonstrated ceiling is far above anything in the instrumental record. The magnetic shield is measurably weakening, faster in some places than the global average. The consequences of those two together are underpriced in public planning. None of that needs a micronova to be true, and all of it comes from the mainstream.

And the part that comes apart

Whether to build is a separate question from whether the mechanism is right, and most arguments about this run the two together. The four scales above rest on the coin toss and the earthquake arithmetic alone, both of which are already planning assumptions for governments and insurers. If the deeper claim is right, the same build covers it. If it is wrong, nothing was wasted.

And the register this is usually read in is the wrong one

Material like this gets filed under dread, and dread produces bunkers, hoarding and a kind of joyless waiting. A bunker helps one household for one event, and helps nobody at all the rest of the time. Simeulue was the other model: ordinary lives, and a rhyme told alongside all the other rhymes, costing nothing until the morning it saved almost every one of them.

Food grown near where it is eaten, water without a pump on a distant grid, power that keeps the fridge going, neighbours who know each other's names: every one of those makes an ordinary Tuesday better.

Twelve thousand years of unusual calm produced farming, cities, writing, music, medicine and every good thing anyone has ever made. That is not a countdown, it is a windfall, and the sane response to a windfall is to use it well and make sure some of it lasts. Knowing the ground moves is what tells you what to build, and how to build it so it is still there afterwards.

The honest position at the end of all this is small. Nobody knows when. Everybody can see it has happened before and will happen again. Between those two facts there is an enormous amount of ordinary, enjoyable, useful work to get on with, and almost none of it requires being frightened.
