Fourteen stock replies

The waffle catalogue

Every one of these has been said in place of an argument. Some reach a defensible conclusion by an indefensible route, and a bad argument for a true conclusion is still a bad argument. Where a real objection is hiding inside one, the better version is marked, and those better versions get made properly on the next page.

  1. The Sun cannot go nova, it has no binary companion.
    The problem
    It answers a claim nobody made. A classical nova needs a dead star with a strange surface stealing hydrogen from a neighbour. The proposed event is not that. Correct physics, wrong target, and it is the single most common thing said about this topic.
    The version that works
    The proposed solar mechanism has no worked model, and borrowing the white dwarf's name does not import the white dwarf's physics.
  2. Micronova is a made-up word.
    The problem
    False. Nature, April 2022. It has been standard vocabulary in astronomy since 2022 and now appears in classification papers.
    The version that works
    The 2022 micronova is a different object, so the word's adoption is not evidence for the solar claim.
  3. Ben Davidson is a lawyer, not a scientist.
    The problem
    An attack on a person, and a tell. Credentials are not evidence. When this turns up in an answer it is usually doing the work a citation should be doing. It is also selectively applied: nobody discounts a correct claim from an amateur astronomer, and the history of the field is full of them.
    The version that works
    Name the claim that is wrong, and show why.
  4. It's pseudoscience.
    The problem
    A verdict standing in for an argument. The word describes a method rather than a conclusion, so it has to be earned by showing the method failing.
    The version that works
    That case can partly be made here. The forecast window has moved more than once, and a forecast that survives every observation by widening has stopped doing work. Say that instead.
  5. There is no evidence.
    The problem
    False, and lazy in a specific way. Miyake events, superflare statistics, Laschamps, the measured field decay, the growing South Atlantic anomaly. All mainstream, all published, all relevant.
    The version that works
    What is usually meant is "there is no evidence for the mechanism", which is defensible and is a different sentence. Say the sentence you mean.
  6. There's no 12,000-year cycle in the record.
    The problem
    Nearly right, and worth stating properly. "No cycle has been demonstrated" and "the record shows there is no cycle" are different claims, and only the first is supportable. The palaeo record is sparse and dating uncertainty grows with depth.
    The version that works
    Check the ladder rung by rung and say what you find. Two accepted, three proposed and never confirmed, two with nothing behind them.
  7. That's just the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, which was debunked.
    The problem
    Two errors. They are separate claims and the micronova argument does not depend on an impact. And "debunked" overstates a live dispute.
    The version that works
    The direction of travel is real: the Greenland crater dated to about 58 million years, the platinum spike reattributed to Icelandic fissure volcanism, and two supporting papers retracted in 2025 and 2026. So leaning on it as corroboration now costs more than it pays.
  8. Scientists would have noticed.
    The problem
    Assumes the conclusion, and the record is against it. Miyake events sat in tree rings until 2012. Superflare frequency on stars like ours was underestimated by one to two orders of magnitude until 2024. The pole motion forced the first out-of-cycle navigation model update in 2019.
    The version that works
    Noticing is a process with a rate, and on this exact topic the rate has been slow and the corrections have all gone the same way.
  9. The field has been weakening for ages, it's normal.
    The problem
    True and incomplete. Normal is the right first read: the field has been through many such episodes.
    The version that works
    Normal is not the same as harmless. What is new is the exposure. An ordinary storm against a weaker field, hitting a grid full of long conductors and large transformers, is not a scenario the geological record can reassure anyone about.
  10. A pole reversal takes thousands of years, nothing to worry about.
    The problem
    Confuses a reversal with an excursion, and overstates the consensus on timing. Excursions are faster and come back to the same polarity, and the literature on how fast a field can change has been revised towards faster, not slower.
    The version that works
    The flip is not the point. The low point on the way is.
  11. Correlation is not causation.
    The problem
    True, and usually deployed to end a conversation rather than advance one. The argument here is explicitly a coincidence-counting argument, so this objection has to do actual work.
    The version that works
    The events were dated and selected by people who already knew what the pattern was meant to be, so the pattern may live in the selection. That is a real objection and close to decisive. Make it.
  12. Peer review would have caught it.
    The problem
    A bad argument in general, and here also a false one. Solar effects on climate oscillations, solar effects on earthquake timing, cosmic-ray effects on cloud and volcanic processes: all have peer-reviewed literature, much of it contested, some of it cited accurately in the frontier material.
    The version that works
    The dispute is over weight and interpretation, not over whether anything is published. Argue the weight.
  13. This is doomsday prepper stuff.
    The problem
    Guilt by association, and it smuggles in a link that is not there. The physics claim and the preparation advice come apart cleanly. Grid hardening, transformer spares, local water, local food and offline capability are justified by the risk of a Carrington-class storm alone, which is already a planning assumption for governments and insurers.
    The version that works
    Notice the move being pulled: the preparation gets dismissed by attacking the physics, and the physics gets dismissed by attacking the preparation. Neither is an argument about either.
  14. It's a YouTube channel.
    The problem
    Not an argument. Where material is published is not a property of whether it is right.
    The version that works
    The books cite real papers with real reference numbers, which anyone can pull. Some support what they are said to support and some carry more weight than the paper claims. Only reading them tells you which, and that reading is the work.

The shape underneath

Ten of fourteen do the same thing.

They attack the wrapper and report it as a refutation of the contents. The catastrophe mythology, the cover-up chapter, the prepping advice, the channel, the author's degree.

Binning a parcel because you recognise the wrapping is fast, and in the wild it is usually right. Which is exactly why it is dangerous here, where the parcel contains claims that can be checked.

The other four are real objections in bad clothes. Dressed properly they are on the next page, and two of them are close to decisive.

The objections that land →

The oldest dismissal of all

"Things have always gone on much as they go on now."

This one is worth separating from the others, because it is not an argument. It is a working assumption that got adopted for reasons that were partly about geology and partly about something else, and it has lost badly, repeatedly, in public.

Geology spent the nineteenth century deciding between two accounts of its own subject. Catastrophism held that the record was cut by sudden violent events. Uniformitarianism held that present rates, applied over long enough, explain everything, and that appealing to catastrophe was a failure of nerve. Uniformitarianism won, and won so completely that catastrophe became a marker of not being a serious person.

Part of that was a real methodological gain. Slow processes had been badly underrated and long time had been badly underrated, and insisting on measurable present-day rates was good discipline. Part of it was that catastrophism was tangled up with scripture, and the field was trying to get away from the flood. Winning an argument against a bad reason for a claim is not the same as showing the claim is false, and geology went on to learn that the hard way, more than once.

Which is the actual point, and it is narrow

None of that makes any particular catastrophic claim true. Bretz being right does not transfer to anyone else, and "they laughed at Galileo" is a bad argument on its own.

What it does establish is that the prior is not neutral. When a field's default setting has been overturned three times in a century, on its biggest questions, by the same kind of claim each time, then treating the next catastrophic proposal as presumptively unserious is not caution. It is a habit with a losing record, and the burden of proof it applies is heavier than the one it applies to itself.

Known

The same point runs the other way and it has to, or it is worthless. Bretz had field evidence: the coulees, the giant ripple marks, the erratics. He was not right because he was bold. The point is about which claims get a fair hearing, not about which claims are true, and a catastrophic proposal with no evidence behind it is exactly as empty as a gradualist one.

The other suppressed idea, and it is the same shelf

Asking who else was out there was once a burning offence.

The charge is that reluctance to talk about life elsewhere is a religious inheritance rather than a scientific position. Half of that holds up under the documents and half does not, and the half that holds up is the older half.

Giordano Bruno was burned in Rome in February 1600. The received handling of this is that the romantic version, that he died for saying there are other inhabited worlds, has been debunked: his real offences were theological, denying the Trinity, the divinity of Christ, the virginity of Mary and transubstantiation, and the cosmology was incidental. Frances Yates wrote that the legend can no longer stand. Michael Crowe called the martyr-for-pluralism story a myth. The Catholic Encyclopedia states he was not condemned for the plurality of inhabited worlds.

Two things about that debunk are worth noticing

The first is what the trial record contains. Across the depositions, six witnesses accused Bruno of believing in many worlds, thirteen separate times, in ten depositions. No other accusation was invoked even half as often. He was formally admonished to abandon the belief and refused. Whatever weight the tribunal finally placed on it, it was the single most repeated charge against him.

The second is who is quoted settling the matter. One of the most-cited sources for "it was not about the cosmology" is the encyclopedia of the institution that held the trial. That is the same class of evidence as a government assessing the papers it has just seized, and this site already declines to treat that as independent in another case. Consistency requires declining it here too.

The honest verdict is that it is genuinely contested, the scholars dismissing it are real scholars and not apologists, and the confident debunk is thinner than its confidence suggests. What needs none of that argument is the simpler fact: a plurality of inhabited worlds was a charge at a heresy trial that ended in an execution. The idea was treated as dangerous by religious authority. That much is in the record whichever way the weighting falls.

Known Disputed

But the modern reticence is not the same animal, and saying so is not a defence of anyone

If the claim is that scientists today avoid the subject of life elsewhere for religious reasons, the evidence runs the other way. The working expectation across astrobiology is that life has probably arisen many times, elsewhere and before us. "Follow the water" is the field's own motto, entire missions are built around it, and Earth-as-a-one-off is a minority position with a name and a small following rather than a consensus.

Where the caution is real it attaches to something narrower: not life, but intelligence, and above all visitation. Those have their own causes, and career risk from a century of stigma is a better explanation than theology.

On the official record of that narrower question, one thing should be said plainly and then stopped. The posture did change: an assessment published in 2021, a dedicated office established the following year, sworn congressional testimony in 2023, and a historical review concluding there is no evidence of recovered non-human technology. That last conclusion was produced by the same institutions the allegations concern, which is precisely the situation this site refuses to treat as independent elsewhere. What is solid: there are unexplained observations sitting in official datasets, there was a long documented period in which reporting them ended careers, and the legislature that commissioned the office is not treating its conclusion as final.
