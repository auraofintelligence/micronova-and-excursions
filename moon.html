An assumption, checked late

The Moon, and what it was assumed to be doing

"The Moon holds our tilt steady" gets used to close down the idea that the Earth was ever oriented very differently. It is true today. It is not a permanent property of the system, it has not always been true to the same degree, and there is a published model of the Moon's own origin in which the Earth is lying almost on its side. Using the first sentence to shut down the question skips all of that.

One

The steadying is a phase, not a property.

What the Moon actually does is make the Earth's spin axis wobble round quickly. That fast wobble keeps the planet out of step with the slower rhythms of its own orbit, and staying out of step is what stops the tilt wandering. Take the Moon away and the calculations go wild: the tilt could roam anywhere between upright and about eighty-five degrees over.

So far, so textbook, and it is the reason our seasons are as mild as they are. But look at what the steadying depends on. The wobble rate is set by how far away the Moon is and how fast the Earth is turning, and both of those have changed enormously. The Moon is drifting away at about the width of a fingernail each year, and the Earth's day has been lengthening the whole time.

Which means the protection has an expiry date, and it is in the literature

As the Moon keeps receding, the wobble slows, and eventually it falls back into step with the orbital rhythms it is currently detuned from. The published estimate is that tidal evolution drives the Earth's spin into the chaotic zone within one and a half to four and a half billion years, after which the tilt can swing past eighty degrees. Same planet, same Moon, no longer stabilised.

"The Moon stabilises the tilt" is a description of the present arrangement. It is not a law, and it was wrong of this ledger to use it as one.

Known

Two

And one leading origin story has the Earth on its side.

This is the part that ought to change how the whole tilt argument is framed, and it is not from the frontier. It is a mainstream answer to a mainstream problem.

The standard account has something roughly Mars-sized hitting the young Earth, with the Moon forming from the debris. It has one stubborn flaw: the Moon's chemistry is almost identical to the Earth's, when a foreign impactor should have left its own signature all through it.

One of the strongest proposed fixes, published in 2016, is an impact that leaves the Earth spinning ferociously fast, under a two and a half hour day, and tipped over by about seventy degrees. The debris then forms a disc around the equator of a planet that is lying on its side, which is where the Moon accretes from. Later, as the lunar orbit widens, it passes through an unstable handover where the Sun's pull takes over from the bulge of the Earth, and that handover sheds the excess spin and sets the Moon's odd orbital tilt.

What that does to the argument

A high-tilt Earth is not an eccentric thing to propose. It sits inside a published solution to the biggest outstanding problem in how the Moon got here, and the reason it is attractive is that it explains the chemistry, the angular momentum and the lunar orbit at once. So when a geologist argues that the tilt was above fifty-four degrees to explain ice at the equator, they are not reaching for something the field finds unthinkable. The field has its own version.

Known Disputed

Held honestly: this is a model of the first few tens of millions of years, and the tilt comes down long before there is anything alive to notice. It does not license a high tilt in the recent past. What it does is retire the idea that a radically differently oriented Earth is inherently silly.

Three

It really did ring for fifty-five minutes.

On 20 November 1969, three days after leaving a seismometer on the surface, Apollo 12 dropped the empty ascent stage of its lander back onto the Moon on purpose, about seventy kilometres from the instrument. The shaking did not stop for over fifty-five minutes. It genuinely surprised the people watching the trace, and the reaction at the time was that the thing had rung like a bell.

The observation is real, it is in the record, and it does not get waved away. Where the story goes wrong is the next step, because hollow is ruled out by the plainest measurement there is: how much the Moon weighs for its size, which is exactly what a mostly-rock body with a small core should weigh, and nothing like a shell.

The real answer is better than the myth

Earth damps a shock quickly because it is warm and wet, and water in the rock soaks up the energy. The Moon has no water to speak of, and its outer few kilometres have been smashed to rubble by billions of years of bombardment. A shock entering that rubble does not travel through cleanly. It scatters among the fractures, ricochets, and arrives back at the instrument from every direction over the better part of an hour. Bone dry and shattered to a great depth, which is why the sound has nowhere to go.

Not a bell because it is hollow. A bell because it is dry, and because everything that ever hit it is still recorded in the cracks.

Four

Sorting the odd from the invented.

There are stories that the Moon was put there deliberately: a ship that arrived, a thing built in place, a watchtower. Fun to think with, and worth keeping separate from the list of things that are genuinely peculiar about it, because the second list is real and does not need the first.

The oddityWhere it stands
It rang for nearly an hourReal, and explained. Dry and deeply fractured, so shock energy scatters instead of damping.
Its chemistry is almost identical to Earth'sReal, and an open problem. A foreign impactor should have left a signature. Fixing it is why the high-tilt origin model exists.
It is enormous for the planet it orbitsReal. Unusual among the rocky planets, and the reason the tilt is steady at all.
It covers the Sun almost exactly during an eclipseReal, and a coincidence of this era. It is drifting away, so the fit is temporary. We happen to be here for it.
It is light for its sizeReal, and accounted for. Made mostly from the Earth's rocky outside rather than its iron middle, which is what the impact models predict.
It is hollowRuled out. Its weight for its size does not permit it.
It was placed there on purposeStory. Enjoyable, and carrying none of the weight above.

The useful move is the same one as everywhere else here. The genuinely strange things about the Moon are strange enough on their own, and each of them is checkable. Leading with the ones that are not checkable is how the checkable ones get discarded along with them.

Five

One pole from the blow, and the other made by it.

An idea worth writing down properly, because it is a real hypothesis and it turns out to sit on established ground: what if a hit produces one strong signature, and whatever appears on the far side of the world is a consequence rather than a second event? It would leave a different kind of mark, and a search looking for a matching pair would keep coming up empty.

The physics behind that is not speculative. It was proposed in the 1970s and it has been mapped on two worlds. Energy from a very large impact travels round and through a planet and converges on the exact opposite point, and where it converges it wrecks the ground from below. On Mercury, directly opposite the huge Caloris basin, sits a stretch of country so strange it was named the weird terrain, and modelling puts around a kilometre of vertical ground movement there. The Moon has the same arrangement opposite the Imbrium basin.

And the shape of the evidence is exactly the asymmetry described

The far side gets no crater. It gets broken, jumbled, dropped ground, which is a different signature entirely and much easier to miss or misread as ordinary geology. Later work also found the effect runs mostly through waves that skim the surface and waves trapped between the core and the outside, rather than straight through the middle, which is the sort of detail that decides whether the far-side mark is sharp or smeared.

So the intuition holds up: one event, two very different records, and the second one not looking like the first. That is a genuine reason a search for symmetry would fail even if the events were real.

What it would take to be more than an intuition

Three things, all of them ordinary work. Whether the same focusing does anything to a planet's magnetism rather than just its surface, which is a question people have already asked about impacts and crustal magnetism. Whether the Earth's own lopsidedness, the weak patch over the South Atlantic against the very different behaviour over the Pacific, has any antipodal structure to it. And whether repeated events would leave a stack of far-side disturbances that could be told apart from ordinary shifting ground.

None of that needs new instruments. It needs someone to look for the asymmetric signature instead of the symmetric one, which is a different search, and as far as can be found nobody has run it for the Earth.
