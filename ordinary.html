The argument that needs no history at all

Catastrophe is ordinary

The sentence carrying most of the weight against everything on this site is that catastrophe is an extraordinary claim. Exoplanet astronomy has quietly made that the wrong category. Nothing on this page requires believing anything at all about the Earth's past, and every line of it is published observation.

The strongest version of the argument on this page

None of it is exotic. It is ordinary, and it is happening somewhere right now.

The usual objection is that catastrophe is an extraordinary claim. Exoplanet astronomy has quietly made that the wrong category, and the reframe costs nothing because every item below is mainstream observation.

Run the list of things a planet is supposed not to have happen to it, and check each one against what the telescopes have actually found in the last decade. The result is not a list of maybes.

Catastrophic planetary events, and their observational status
The eventWhere it stands elsewhere
A planet thrown out of its systemFree-floating planets are found by microlensing and directly by JWST, and both simulation and observation suggest they may dramatically outnumber bound planets below Earth mass. Ejection by scattering, by a passing star, or by a binary companion is a standard outcome, not a freak one. Losing your star may be the commonest fate a small planet has.
Endless day on one face, endless night on the otherTidal locking is the expected condition for terrestrial planets around M dwarfs, and M dwarfs are the most common star in the galaxy. Permanent day and permanent night is arguably the normal planetary condition here, and Earth's daily rotation is the odd case. There is a whole literature on the climate of those worlds.
A star flaring hard enough to rework a planet's airModelled in detail and constrained by observation. Flares from an active M dwarf drive ozone up by a factor of about twenty against a quiet star, and energise the middle atmosphere enough to raise night-side winds by tens of metres per second. Work on how young-star flares shaped early exo-Earth atmospheres was still being published in 2026.
Two planets hitting each otherObserved. The afterglow of a giant planet collision has been caught, and further collision evidence was reported in 2026. Per system the largest impacts are rare; across a few hundred billion systems, rare is a busy schedule.

Which moves the burden without needing a single historical claim

Every one of those is ordinary planetary physics with a paper behind it. So the sentence "planets do not have things like that happen to them" is simply false, and it was the sentence doing most of the work.

What is actually unusual is not the catastrophe. It is our stretch without one, and that is a statement about the length of the record and the length of the species, not about how the universe behaves.

One more, and this one is a clock rather than a catastrophe. The stars really were in different places, and it needs no event at all. The Earth's axis wobbles through a full circle in about 25,800 years, so the pole moves against the sky and the pole star changes with it: Thuban held the position around five thousand years ago, Polaris holds it now, Vega takes it in roughly thirteen thousand years. The constellation rising before the Sun at a given season shifts through the whole zodiac across the same cycle. Anyone keeping records over long enough sees the sky reorganise itself, exactly as described, and the mechanism is clockwork. A mainstream cycle of about twenty-six thousand years, sitting in the same range as the deep-time claims, is worth far more to that argument than any disputed artefact.

The same argument, run all the way down

Fiction's list of ways a world ends, checked against the journals.

Liu Cixin's game inside The Three-Body Problem kills its civilisation over and over, in a different way each time. Read as a list of failure modes rather than as a story, almost every one has since been photographed happening to something real.

Fictional planetary endings and their observational status
How the world ends in the gameWhere it stands in the journals
Orbits nobody can predict, stable spells that end without warningThe three-body problem is genuinely chaotic with no general closed-form solution, which is a mathematical result rather than a plot device. Planets are found in double and triple star systems, and the dynamics are as fragile as advertised.
The planet falls into a sunCaught in the act. A survey picked up the optical flash of a star swallowing its own planet, with dust thrown off as the planet spiralled into the star's outer layers. It is no longer a scenario.
Everything torn up and swallowed at the endThis is the ordinary end state, and the number is the striking part. Somewhere between a quarter and a half of all white dwarfs carry iron, calcium and magnesium in their atmospheres that has no business being there and can only have come from eaten planetary material. X-rays have been seen from that debris heated to a million degrees as it lands. Around one star, planetary fragments have been watched breaking up and transiting in real time.
Burned by the sun coming too closeObserved. Planets are found evaporating under their star, losing mass, with the vapour recondensing into dust trails behind them.
Frozen when the suns go far awayFree-floating planets, ejected from their systems, found by microlensing and directly by telescope. Possibly more numerous than the ones that kept their star.
Thrown out of the system entirelySame population. Ejection is a standard outcome of the dynamics rather than a freak accident.
Space itself collapsed from three dimensions to twoFiction, and this site will say so rather than blur it. No observational analogue. What is real and separate is the holographic principle, a serious and heavily worked line in theoretical physics, which is a different claim about how information in a volume can be encoded on its boundary. Keeping those apart is the whole method.

Which is the point, and it is stronger than any historical claim

The list was written as invention. It reads now as a catalogue. Every entry but the last has a paper behind it, and the last one is marked because marking it is what makes the rest usable.

A universe this size does not offer these as possibilities. It runs them constantly, everywhere, and has done for billions of years. The only genuinely unusual thing in the picture is a planet that has gone a long time without its turn, which is a fact about the length of our record.

On water, the case is as strong as it sounds and needs no hedging: hydrogen and water are the two most abundant molecules in the universe, and water makes up around seventy per cent of interstellar ice. It is in molecular clouds, in the discs planets form from, in comets, and in the atmospheres of other worlds. Water is not a lucky local ingredient. It is one of the things the universe is mostly made of.

On life, one honest half-step back, and one correction that runs the other way

The half-step: physics does not demand life, because nobody can calculate the rate. The ingredients are demonstrably everywhere, the conditions are common, and the step from chemistry to something that copies itself has happened once in the only sample anyone has. Without a mechanism you cannot put a number on it, and a claim with no number in it cannot be a demand.

The correction, which is larger: "a fluke on Earth" is not what the field thinks either. The working expectation across astrobiology is that life has probably arisen many times across the universe, and before us. The genuinely contested part is not whether life is out there, it is how often it gets complicated, and how often it gets technological. Anyone arguing against a scientific consensus that Earth is a one-off is arguing with something that mostly is not there.

An evidence ledger for one contested argument. Not a verdict, and not an endorsement: the corrections run in both directions on purpose.
