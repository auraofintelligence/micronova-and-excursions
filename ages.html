The question underneath all of it

The ages, and why they all end

We are the survivors of a lineage running back millions of years. Of everything that lineage could have chosen to keep and carry, across all those generations, an enormous share of what actually made it through is an account of a world ending. That is a strange inheritance, and it wants explaining rather than filing away.

One

The suns, counted.

The clearest version comes from the Nahua peoples of central Mexico, and it is literally a count of suns. Not ages named for kings or gods. Ages of the sun, each one ended.

The Five Suns
SunNameHow it ended
FirstFour JaguarThe people were devoured
SecondFour WindTorn apart by hurricanes
ThirdFour RainA rain of fire
FourthFour WaterA flood
FifthFour MovementThe current one. It ends in earthquakes.

The middle three in a row run wind, fire from the sky, then water. The last row places this tradition inside a series rather than at the end of one, and it names the way this one goes.

Known

Two

The worlds, and a warning about the source.

The Hopi account that circulates most widely has four worlds, the first ended by fire, the second by ice, the third by flood, and the fourth the one we are in. Fire, ice, water, in that order, which is the same set as the suns with the jaguars traded for cold.

And here the provenance matters more than the pattern

Almost every popular retelling of that traces back to a single book published in 1963, presented as an account of Hopi tradition drawn from statements by twenty-seven elders. It has been contested ever since. Hopi people have publicly objected to it, groups have sought meetings with the authors over it, and anthropologists have criticised how it was assembled. Some of the objection is about accuracy and some is about a book bringing a stream of outsiders onto the mesas looking for something to join.

So the honest handling is: this is what is widely reported, the source is one contested book rather than the tradition speaking for itself, and the people whose tradition it is have said so. Repeating it as though it were straight from the Hopi is the same error as repeating a figure without checking it, and it does more harm because it is somebody's living religion.

That caution generalises. The most circulated version of a tradition is often not the tradition. It is a book about the tradition, written by an outsider, and the difference is exactly the sort of thing this ledger exists to keep visible.

Three

The same shape, a long way apart.

Set the accounts beside each other and a structure shows up that is worth naming precisely, because the precise version is more interesting than the loose one.

  • The Norse have the world going dark and bitterly cold, the sun blackening, the land sinking into a roaring sea, and then coming back. Ends and begins again.
  • Buddhist teaching includes a sequence in which the sun goes through stages of burning, and separately a version in which the oceans fall and the world is remade. Ends and begins again.
  • Hindu tradition counts ages in cycles, each running down and turning over. Ends and begins again.
  • Zoroastrian teaching has the sun becoming more hidden and spotted, and the days and the year growing shorter.
  • Across Christian and Islamic material, a darkened sun, a red moon, the earth swaying, and in one strand the sun rising in the wrong place.

The structural point, which is not the obvious one

The striking feature is not that everyone has an ending. It is that so many of them have an ending and then another beginning, in a series, with this one numbered. A single final apocalypse is the unusual version, not the common one. Most of these traditions describe a world that has been wound up and restarted before, which is a different claim and a much more specific one.

Four

Why the surviving record is nearly all endings.

This is the question worth asking, and it has an answer that is better for the argument than the usual framing manages, because it supplies a mechanism instead of a coincidence.

Keeping an account alive by mouth is expensive. It costs time, attention, ceremony, and people willing to carry it and be held to it. Nobody pays that across three hundred generations for something merely interesting. What earns the cost is information that keeps people alive: where the water came to, what the sky did before it came, which ground held and which ground did not, how long it lasted, what to do.

So the bias is the mechanism, not a flaw in it

If oral tradition works as survival technology, then a record dominated by catastrophe is exactly what it should look like. The stories are not disproportionately about disaster because our ancestors were morbid. They are about disaster because that is the only class of information worth the price of carrying it for ten thousand years. The catastrophe bias in the record is evidence that the record was doing a job.

You do not keep a thing alive for three hundred generations because it is a good story. You keep it because someone might need it.

Five

And the same argument cuts the other way.

Which has to be said in the same breath, because it is the objection that will come and it is a fair one.

That selection also explains the universality without needing one global event. Every people has flood accounts because every people lives near water and water kills. Every people has an underworld because everyone buries their dead and everyone has caves and everyone has felt the ground move. A story type can be near-universal because the experience is near-universal, and that requires no single catastrophe at all.

Which is why specific beats universal, every time

Twenty-one Aboriginal accounts, each describing its own stretch of coast going under, matching the sea-level rise at that place, are far stronger evidence than a thousand cultures having a flood story. The universal version is compatible with almost anything. The specific version is a dated, located, checkable claim that came out right. That is where the case actually lives, and it is a better case than the one usually made for it.

So the honest position on this material is neither of the two on offer. Not that the shared shape of the stories proves a shared event. And not that they are only stories, which the Australian work has now put beyond arguing. They are a record with a known bias, a known mechanism, and a demonstrated reach, and the work is matching individual accounts to individual events rather than counting how many cultures have a flood.
