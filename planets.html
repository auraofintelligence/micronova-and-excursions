The coincidence-counting argument, counted

The other planets

If something outside the Solar System were driving change here, it should be driving change everywhere, and the claim is that it is. Nearly every planet does show documented change. What follows is each one against the observations, because this list is the most checkable thing in the whole argument and nobody seems to have checked it in public.

One

Two that hold, and are genuinely unexplained.

These are not fringe reports. They are mission results, published, with the researchers involved saying plainly that they do not know the cause.

Venus is spinning its atmosphere faster

2006 to 2013
  • Observed → cloud-top winds rose from around 300 kilometres per hour to nearly 400 over six years
    Known Tracked by hand across more than 45,000 cloud features and by computer across another 350,000, from a spacecraft in orbit. The lead researcher's own words: an enormous increase in an already high rotation rate, never before observed on Venus, and not yet understood. That is roughly a third faster, which is the figure the frontier material quotes, and the figure is right.

Neptune's temperature went the wrong way, twice

2003 to 2020
  • Observed → the upper atmosphere cooled by at least 7 degrees through to 2018, and then the south pole warmed by about 11 degrees in two years
    Known Published in 2022 from two decades of heat imaging. Polar warming like it had never been seen on Neptune before, and it reversed the cooling that came first. The cause is stated as unknown, and the candidates offered are seasonal chemistry, ordinary weather variability, or a response to the eleven-year solar cycle. Worth noticing which of those three is on a mainstream candidate list for an outer-planet temperature anomaly.

Two

Two that are real, and have a good local answer.

Real changes. Not mysterious ones, though in both cases something about the size of the change was a surprise.

Uranus went from bland to stormy. When a spacecraft passed in 1986 it was almost featureless. Since then it has thrown up storms the size of continents. The answer is that Uranus is tipped almost onto its side and takes eighty-four Earth years to go round, so its seasons are extreme and glacially slow, and sunlight reached latitudes in 2007 that had been dark for decades. It also has no real internal heat of its own, so what the Sun does to it shows up more clearly than on any other giant planet. That last part is worth keeping.

Pluto's atmosphere tripled while it moved away from the Sun. Twelve measurements between 1988 and 2016 show the pressure roughly doubling every decade, through a period when the sunlight reaching it fell by a fifth. Counterintuitive, and explained: a two-hundred-and-forty-eight-year orbit means the surface lags a long way behind, so it kept releasing nitrogen even as the light faded. Since then the pressure has begun dropping again as that nitrogen refreezes, which models predicted, though the drop is larger than they predicted.

And a very recent one, which cuts the other way

Fifteen hours of infrared observation of Uranus in January 2025, published in 2026, found the upper atmosphere has kept cooling across the last thirty years. Still hot at around 150 degrees, and lower than it was in the late twentieth century. So one of the planets on the list is undergoing a documented, sustained, decades-long change in the opposite direction to the one the argument wants.

Three

One to drop, and one to state properly.

Mars appears twice in the frontier material and the two claims are in very different shape.

The claimWhat is actually the case
Mars is warming, faster than EarthNot supported, and this one has a history. It traces to a 2007 paper about dust blowing around and changing how much sunlight the surface reflects, in patches. The predicted effect was 0.65 of a degree in total across about twenty years, not per year, and the journal had to publish a correction because the wording was read that way. It is a regional dust effect, not a planet warming, and it has been circulated as evidence of solar-system-wide heating for nearly two decades.
Mars is seismically active, unexpectedly soSupported, and interesting. A lander recorded over 1,300 marsquakes. The largest, in May 2022, was magnitude 4.7 and released as much energy as every other quake it had catalogued put together. Follow-up work ruled out a meteorite and put it down to stress released inside the crust. Mars is more seismically alive than expected.

Note the difference in kind. The seismic finding says Mars is more active than we thought, which is a statement about our previous ignorance. It does not say Mars is becoming more active, because there was no earlier baseline to compare against. Those two get run together and they should not be.

Four

The test that decides it.

Take the whole list as read. Every change on it is real. The question is whether it is a pattern, and there is a way to ask that does not depend on liking the answer.

Do the changes point the same way?

A single external driver should leave a coherent fingerprint. Not identical everywhere, because each world has its own weather, but the same sign. That is not what the list shows. Uranus has been cooling in its upper atmosphere for thirty years. Neptune cooled and then had one pole warm sharply. Venus sped up. Pluto's pressure rose and is now falling. These are not one thing happening in several places. They are several things happening.

The second problem is arithmetic. "Almost every planet is changing" needs a denominator, and nobody supplies one. How many bodies were looked at, how many showed nothing, and how much change would be normal for a world we have only watched for a few decades? Without those numbers the observation cannot be told apart from what any improving survey turns up when it points better instruments at more things.

And a caution that cuts at both sides

Uranus takes eighty-four years to go round, Neptune a hundred and sixty-five, Pluto two hundred and forty-eight. We have been watching most of these worlds for a fraction of one of their years. On a baseline that short, "this is seasonal" and "this is changing" are extremely hard to tell apart, and reaching confidently for either one is reaching past the data. That is the same problem as judging the Sun from a hundred and seventy years of records, and it is not a point that belongs to one side of this argument.

The list is real. The pattern is not established. And "not established" is doing different work here than "explained away", because most of these have not been explained.

Five

What has happened since the books were written.

The frontier texts stop in 2022, and the picture has moved since. Three of the changes run in favour of taking the Sun more seriously, not less.

  • The forecast for this solar cycle was wrong, in the direction of underestimating it. The official 2019 panel called for a weak cycle peaking around a sunspot number of 115 in mid-2025. It was revised upward in 2023, and the actual peak came in October 2024 at about 161. Counts reached a twenty-three year high, and an X9 flare arrived that October. The consensus underestimated the Sun again, in public, within the last two years.
  • The strongest geomagnetic storm in over twenty years arrived in May 2024. The first severe-category storm since 2003. About half of the ten thousand satellites in low orbit manoeuvred, the largest such movement on record, one was dragged down eleven days early, and the ionosphere emptied out to a record degree. No blackouts, and grid operators spent the weekend managing voltage. A live demonstration of the exposure, and it was nowhere near the top of the scale.
  • The weak patch in the Earth's field is still growing, and unevenly. Eleven years of satellite data to 2025: expanded by an area near half of continental Europe since 2014, and weakening faster since 2020 in the stretch south-west of Africa. The lead author's framing is that it is not one block, and behaves differently towards Africa than towards South America. Longest continuous monitoring of the field yet made.
