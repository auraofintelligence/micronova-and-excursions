Tests, not verdicts

What would settle it

Both sides of this argument mostly assert. The useful move is to name the observation that would change a mind, then say whether it can be reached with instruments that already exist. Several of these can be done now, cheaply, by anyone with a laptop and public data.

Five that would strengthen it

The work worth doing.

1

A third confirmed excursion at the right spacing

  1. The two confirmed events sit about 6,500 years apart.
  2. A properly dated third at roughly 28,000 or 48,000 years, from more than one continent.
  3. Dated blind, by people not told what the answer is meant to be.
  4. That moves the clock from asserted to arguable. Ordinary palaeomagnetism, nothing exotic.
2

Tree-ring spikes landing on the excursions

  1. Two catalogues, built independently, both improving.
  2. Check whether the confirmed carbon spikes cluster on the low points of the field, or scatter.
  3. The strongest candidate event sits close to the claimed 12,000-year rung.
  4. One coincidence so far, and a checkable one.
3

A dust measurement at the Sun

  1. The claim needs material to accumulate.
  2. Spacecraft already measure the dust near the Sun directly.
  3. Publish a required accumulation rate and compare it.
  4. It would either survive contact or die on it. The highest-value single piece of work available, and its absence is conspicuous.
4

A worked energy budget

  1. How much material, at what density, over what interval.
  2. What it would take to build a layer the Sun can shed.
  3. What the ejection would look like through a telescope.
  4. Numbers that could be wrong. Nothing else moves this out of story territory.
5

The earthquake correlation, replicated

  1. A 2020 paper reported solar wind density tracking large quakes at one day's lag.
  2. It drew formal responses and the USGS does not support it.
  3. Run it again on a fresh catalogue, with the method registered before the data is touched.
  4. Decisive either way, and a pure data exercise.

Four that would weaken it

The work nobody on that side wants done.

A position worth holding names these too. If none of them can be stated, the position is not a hypothesis.

  • Blind redating of the empty rungs. Take the 30,000, 24,000, 18,000 and 12,000-year slots and have them assessed by people who are not told what the answer should be. If the two weakest stay unsupported under a dedicated search, the ladder is finished as evidence.
  • The decay staying straight. The claim needs acceleration. The best current model of the core says the decline continues at roughly the present rate for decades. If the field keeps falling near five per cent a century and the pole keeps slowing through the 2030s, the "happening now" case runs out of instrument readings.
  • An audit of the misses. Count the nearby stars towards the galactic centre that did not flare, and the Solar System bodies that showed no change. If the reported hits sit inside what a growing search would turn up by chance, the coincidence argument loses its denominator.
  • RUN: a ceiling on shed mass, from the orbits. tools/shed_mass_bound.py. Lose mass slowly, as the Sun does now, and the orbits simply widen. Lose it in far less than one lap and the planet keeps the speed it had, which is now too fast for a lighter Sun to hold in a circle, so it swings out wide and comes back: the circle becomes an oval, and a bigger shed makes a longer oval. Result: shedding is bounded, not forbidden. Earth's orbit already stretches and relaxes on its own over tens of thousands of years, tugged by Jupiter and Venus, so the question is how large a shed could hide inside that natural wobble. The answer is about five per cent of the Sun, which is roughly eighteen thousand Earths' worth of material. Shed half the Sun and the planet leaves for good. So the orbits permit an event enormous in absolute terms and small as a fraction, and that number had not been written down by either side. Also caught, running it: the accumulated orbital expansion over the system's life is about 62,000 km, not the 11,000 this site had published, and the widely quoted "7,000 miles" contradicts the 1.5 cm per year it is printed beside.
  • Is the eruption longitude actually independent of where Earth is? This is the assumption underneath the coin-toss calculation below, it has never been flagged in that argument by anyone, and it is checkable this afternoon on public data. Take a CME source-location catalogue and plot the distribution of source longitude relative to the Sun-Earth line. If it is flat, the one-in-eight figure stands. If there is a bump toward or away from Earth, the number is wrong in whichever direction the bias runs, and something interesting is going on. The context that makes it worth doing rather than assuming: planetary influence on solar activity is a live published question, with the Venus, Earth and Jupiter alignment period of 11.07 years tracking the eleven-year cycle, tidally synchronised dynamo models in mainstream journals, and a 2022 review of the whole planetary theory. The open part is the mechanism, since the tidal forces are very weak. Note the models are about when the Sun is active rather than which face erupts, so this test asks a question they do not answer.
  • RUN: how often is the Sun actually pointed at us? tools/coin_toss.py. A large eruption leaves from wherever its active region happens to sit. The arrival literature puts the Earth-reaching band at about eighty degrees of solar longitude, with roughly three in five of the full-halo events launched from inside it actually arriving. That is a fifth of the Sun, times three fifths, so about one in eight per big eruption, and the Sun turns once a month so the band comes round again and again. Fold that into the published Carrington-class rate of ten to twelve per cent per decade and the chance of at least one direct hit is 65 to 72 per cent over a century. More likely than not, on entirely mainstream numbers, with nothing contested anywhere in it. Held honestly: the window is longitude only, latitude moves it with cycle phase, and the per-decade rate has been revised in both directions, so treat one in eight as an order of magnitude. At the low published end a century still lands near one in twenty.
  • RUN: is there actually a cycle in the excursion dates? tools/cadence_test.py. Rayleigh test on circular phase, over the seven proposed excursions of the last 41 kyr, including the unconfirmed ones because excluding them would stack the test. Fold 41,000 years of history up like a concertina every 6,000 years and see whether the events land on top of each other. They do not, at either claimed period. They land all over the fold. Then the honest trap, checked: let the search hunt for any rhythm at all between 2,000 and 25,000 years and it finds a lovely one at 7,660 years, with the events lining up neatly. So the same hunt was run across twenty thousand sets of seven completely made-up dates. It found an equally convincing cycle in pure noise about one time in six. Seven dates spread across 41,000 years will always fit some rhythm if you go looking. Finding one proves nothing. That is a fact about having only seven dates, not a fact about the Sun. Fixed seed, reproducible, and it tests the clock alone, which is the rung the argument needs least.

    And the ellipse does not heal, which matters. Two things round an orbit off again: tides, and drag from surrounding gas. Tides at Earth's distance from the Sun are so feeble the job would take far longer than the universe has existed, and the gas that rounded the planets off in the first place has been gone for over four billion years. So an imposed stretch stays. Every shed the Sun has ever had is still written into the orbit, which makes the five per cent a budget for the whole of history rather than an allowance per event. Two qualifiers, both tightening it further: repeats arriving at random points in the orbit wander rather than stack, so a hundred events give roughly ten times one event; and Earth's orbital stretch is calculable from the pull of the whole planetary system and the calculation matches what is measured, leaving little unaccounted room for a shed to sit in. Said carefully, because the loose version of this sentence is wrong: what is calculated is the orbit. Why the climate responds most strongly to the weakest of the three orbital signals is a named and unsolved problem, and the bound here does not depend on it. The orbital calculation also runs out at about fifty to sixty million years back, beyond which the orbit cannot be retrodicted at all.
  • Put the disputed spherules on an oxygen isotope plot, with the reference stated honestly. Solar system oxygen falls into two reservoirs that are far apart: the Genesis mission found the Sun roughly 4 per cent enriched in oxygen-16 relative to rocky bodies, which is a structural split rather than a small drift, and is read as photolytic self-shielding during the system's formation. So a spherule population can be placed on one side or the other, and a terrestrial impact-melt origin predicts the planetary side. The result would be asymmetric and that is what makes it worth running: a planetary answer settles little, since impact melt is the expected default, while a solar-reservoir answer would be genuinely hard to explain and nobody appears to have looked.

    What this test cannot do, stated up front. The solar reference is a snapshot: Genesis collected solar wind for 27 months, against 4.56 billion years of stellar life. Worse for the clean version, the field already knows the surface composition has moved. Thermal diffusion, gravitational settling and radiative acceleration have depleted elements heavier than helium in the outer convection zone by around 13 per cent from protosolar values over the Sun's life, and helium by about 15, which is why published photospheric abundances carry a modelled correction back to protosolar before anyone uses them. Solar models with protosolar accretion and turbulent mixing were still being published in 2025. So "the Sun's surface composition is fixed" is not the mainstream position, and the reservoir split is the discriminator rather than a fixed solar line. The test places material in a reservoir. It cannot date it, cannot distinguish the Sun from anything else sharing that reservoir, and would need the settling and mixing corrections carried explicitly rather than assumed away.
  • Join the iron and the bubble, because they give the same two dates. Iron-60 in deep-sea sediments, lunar samples and Antarctic ice records nearby supernovae in two windows: 3.2 to 1.7 million years ago, and around 6 to 8 million. Separately, heliospheric modelling published in 2024 puts the Solar System crossing a cold dense cloud that compressed its protective bubble inside the Earth orbit at about 2 million years, with another likely episode near 7 million. Two unrelated methods, the same two dates. It is not clear that either field has deliberately compared them. The check is bounded: line the isotope arrival curves up against the modelled compression episodes and ask whether that is coincidence or one event seen twice. Public data on both sides.
  • Check the sky the petroglyphs imply. The plasma-physics reading of rock art, published in IEEE Transactions on Plasma Science in 2003 and 2007, argues the carved forms match laboratory high-current discharges and implies a solar wind one to two orders of magnitude stronger than today. Argue about the drawings forever and nothing settles. But a solar wind a hundred times stronger, sustained, is not subtle: it should leave a cosmogenic isotope signature in ice cores and tree rings, in the very archives that already carry the Miyake events. Look in the millennia implied. Either it is there or it is not. As far as can be found nobody has run it from either direction in twenty years, which is a strange thing to leave sitting. Set out here.
  • Split the lunar month, because gravity and the magnetic tail disagree about it. The gravitational tide peaks twice a month, at new moon and full moon equally, so any purely gravitational effect on earthquakes must be symmetric. But the Moon only passes through the Earth's magnetic tail at full moon, and the measured lunar modulation of geomagnetic activity is itself lopsided: about 4% below average for the seven days before full and 4% above for the seven days after. So take a public earthquake catalogue, subtract what the tidal stress model already accounts for, and look at the residual by lunar phase. Flat residual kills the magnetic idea. A residual leaning to the days after full moon is something gravity cannot produce. Free data, no new instruments, and the two relevant literatures appear never to have been joined. Set out here.
  • Look for the asymmetric signature, not the symmetric one. A very large impact focuses energy on the exact opposite point of a planet and wrecks the ground there from below. It is mapped on Mercury opposite the Caloris basin and on the Moon opposite Imbrium, and the far side gets broken jumbled ground rather than a matching crater. So a search for paired evidence would fail even where the events were real. Three checkable questions follow: whether that focusing touches a planet's magnetism as well as its surface, whether the Earth's own lopsided field has any antipodal structure, and whether repeated events would stack a distinguishable far-side record. Ordinary work, no new instruments, and as far as can be found nobody has run it for the Earth. Set out here.
  • Isotope provenance, done properly. The short-lived heavy elements are a load-bearing pillar and the least checked in public. Measured concentrations, in dated layers, against the known background from space dust and cosmic rays. If the anomalies sit inside background, the pillar goes.

And three that settle nothing

Worth naming anyway.

Not tests

Waiting. "The event will come" and "the event has not come yet" are both consistent with the claim as it currently stands. Time passing is not testing it.

Counting subscribers, citations or credentials. On either side.

Another documentary, or another rebuttal video. The argument is not short of rhetoric.

The method underneath

Nobody has to be trusted.

The proposal is not that anyone should take the frontier's word or the consensus's word. It is that a claim earns a stronger label when someone turns it into a calculation anyone can re-run against the same public numbers, and then runs it. That is unusually available here, because the inputs are free.

The frontier says the mainstream will not test this. The mainstream says there is nothing to test. Both positions are comfortable, and both are cheaper than doing the sums and publishing what comes out.

The stance that holds

Not belief, and not dismissal. This is checkable, the data is free, and nobody has checked it properly.
